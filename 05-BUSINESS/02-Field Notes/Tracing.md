  ## Trace 1: Original price and scope

  The object is:

  > “We will perform this defined work for this price.”

  ### Step 3 — Estimate: created

  Known:

  - Owner/estimator defines the scope.
  - Owner calculates the price.
  - The calculation may involve paper, estimating software, web searches, CAD, experience, and supplier information.

  We need to distinguish three things:

  1. The calculation used to arrive at the price.
  2. The written scope describing what is included.
  3. The final number offered to the customer.

  Questions: 1. SMALL BUSINESS, 2. MEDIUM, 3. LARGE

  - What is the final estimate physically created in: paper, Word, Excel, QuickBooks, estimating software, email, or something else?
	1. Small business usually have just a notebook.
	2. Medium use QuickBooks usually
	3. Large estimation software
  - Are price and scope written on the same document?
	1. Small companies usually write scope into the contract and type it out in Microsoft Word or similar
	2. Medium will have scope on contract and in software program
	3. large almost everything is digital somewhere
  - Are exclusions and assumptions written down?
	1. Usually exclusions are but hardly ever assumptions
	2. same 
	3. If you have good employees yes
  - Is the underlying calculation saved, or only the final number?
	1. Only the final number most is just done off the amount of days you think job will take and materials
	2. Yes in application usually, but smaller mid-size companies no
	3. YES ALWAYS
  - Is there one authoritative copy?
	1. If the owner is good, but it rarely lasts longer than the job
	2. Yes 
	3. YES

  ### Step 4 — Estimate discussion: first transfer

  The estimate is sent or explained to the customer.

  Potential copies now include:

  - Contractor’s original estimate
  - Email attachment or email body
  - Customer’s downloaded/printed copy
  - Notes from the phone conversation
  - Each person’s memory of what was discussed

  This is the first major drift point.

  Questions:

  - Is the exact written estimate sent to the customer?
	1. The one the owner decides on yes
	2. With markup yes
	3. With markup yes
  - Is the customer discussing the same document while on the phone?
	1. Yes if they are able to access online
	2. again if the call was scheduled correctly
	3. MOST OF THE TIME YES
  - If the customer requests a change, is the original estimate revised?
	1. NEVER, lucky if it is written down
	2. No, but if contractor is good a change order goes through software
	3. Yes, but in the way of change order and they are still often lost or not recorded correctly
  - Does the revised estimate receive a new version or date?
	1. N/A
	2. Never
	3. If done correctly
  - Is the old version retained?
	1. yes as nothing changed
	2. yes
	3. yes and documented well most of the time
  - Are verbal clarifications added to the written scope?
	1. No
	2. not likely
	3. sometimes, if you want to get paid you better
  - Does the customer sign or otherwise approve the final version?
	1. no
	2. hardly ever
	3. YES or equivlent

  If a phone conversation changes the understanding but the document is not updated, the scope begins drifting here.

  Written estimate
      ↓
  Phone discussion
      ├─ document updated → price/scope may remain intact
      └─ document not updated → written scope and shared understanding diverge

  ### Step 5 — Job preparation: operational re-entry

  The accepted scope must become:

  - Material orders
  - Tool requirements
  - Permit actions
  - Crew instructions
  - Schedule
  - Job budget

  Questions:

  - Does anyone work directly from the accepted estimate?
	1. Owner puts things on the time line for start and orders needed material for delivery
	2. Yes same process usually general site supervisor deals with this though
	3. Yes Project Manager puts the correct things in motion for start date
  - Is its scope copied into a work order or project plan?
	1. In the owners head
	2. In a good company the plans are shared on paper and digitally
	3. YES ALL THE TIME
  - Is the material list copied or rebuilt from memory?
	1. YES
	2. Not usually, but often if things are needed as the job progresses
	3. Not likely but it does happen sometimes when things on-site are not tracked correctly
  - Is the budget copied into a job-cost record?
	1. NO
	2. Often QuickBooks
	3. Yes not sure
  - Are workers shown the written scope?
	1. Owner wrote it lol
	2. Not often on smaller projects but the larger the job yes
	3. Usually
  - Do they know what is specifically excluded?
	1. Yes
	2. This is often not communicated great at first
	3. Yes should be in software and done on walk throughs

  A major distortion can occur if the estimate is a sales document but the crew works from verbal instructions.

  Customer-approved scope
      ↓
  Owner interprets it
      ↓
  Materials and crew instructions are recreated
      ↓
  The crew may receive only part of what was originally promised

  ### Step 6 — Work performed: reality changes the baseline

  During construction, the original scope encounters:

  - Unknown conditions
  - Customer requests
  - Material substitutions
  - Schedule changes
  - Additional labor
  - Work performed differently than estimated

  Questions:

  - Is completed work checked against the original scope?
	1. Often just against the clients eyes.
	2. Same as 1 but they also do check against scope more often than smaller.
	3. Yes everything is usually signed off and logged as completed
  - Can the crew see the latest approved version?
	1. NO
	2. not likely
	3. The foreman can usually but change orders sometimes move very slowly through the channels
  - Are additional tasks separated from original-scope tasks?
	1. NO
	2. more often than small business'
	3. YES IF THEY WANT TO GET PAID
  - Are labor and materials recorded against the original estimate?
	1. no
	2. usually no
	3. YES
  - Can the owner identify whether an overrun came from a bad estimate, changed scope, rework, or an unknown condition?
	1. Usually blame themselves
	2. sometimes they know others just blame themselves for bad estimate
	3. YES 90% of work is tracked digitally if not more

  Without this separation, the original price survives only as a number—not as a controlled relationship between price and work.

  ### Step 6a — Changes: baseline breaks

  A change occurs, but the original estimate is not formally amended.

  The process becomes:

  Original price + original scope
                ↓
         unexpected condition
                ↓
          verbal discussion
                ↓
             work proceeds
                ↓
  Original document no longer describes the actual job

  At this point, at least four different versions of truth may exist:

  - Original written estimate
  - Contractor’s understanding of the changed scope
  - Customer’s understanding of the changed scope
  - Work actually performed

  Unless the change is documented, there is no authoritative new price/scope baseline.

  ### Step 7 — Final invoice: final re-entry

  Questions:

  - Is the final invoice generated directly from the accepted estimate?
	1. Yes with a verbal request for extras included, but never written
	2. Yes, most of the time
	3. YES
  - Is the original contract price manually typed again?
	1. No
	2. No
	3. No
  - Are deposits and progress payments automatically deducted?
	1. The owner does with pen and paper usually
	2. yes
	3. YES
  - Are changes added as separate line items?
	1. no
	2. not usually but if they are good with QuickBooks yes
	3. YES
  - Are verbal changes reconstructed from memory?
	1. Yes
	2. Yes
	3. No
  - Does the invoice show original contract amount, approved changes, payments, and balance?
	1. NO
	2. sometimes
	3. YES
  - Is the final invoice reconciled against the work actually completed?
	1. somewhat by client
	2. usually by client as well, but sometimes on bigger jobs yes
	3. YES
  - Can every added dollar be connected to written customer approval?
	1. NEVER
	2. not likely
	3. higher chances but doubtful still

  A healthy final reconciliation would look like:

  Original approved price
  + written approved change orders
  - deposits and progress payments
  = final amount due

  Your mapped process appears closer to:

  Original estimate
  + remembered extra work
  - payments already received
  ± final negotiation
  = whatever the contractor can collect

  ### Step 7 — Payment: intact, distorted, or lost

  The original price reaches payment intact only if:

  - The customer approved the written estimate.
  - No undocumented changes occurred.
  - The invoice carries forward the same number.
  - Deposits are accurately reconciled.
  - The customer pays without renegotiating.

  Based on your map, the price can be distorted by:

  - Verbal estimate revisions
  - Unwritten scope clarifications
  - Unrecorded extra work
  - Forgotten material or labor costs
  - Customer negotiation after completion
  - Contractor reluctance to demand the agreed amount
  - Manual invoice recreation
  - Partial or late payment

  The information object may survive, but its authority does not.

  ## Trace 2: Change-order approval

  The object is:

  > “The customer approved this additional work, its additional price, and any schedule effect.”

  ### Step 6a — Unknown condition discovered

  Known:

  - Builder discovers unexpected work.
  - Builder tells the owner.
  - Owner calls the customer.

  Questions:

  - Does the builder photograph the condition?
  - Does the builder write down what was found?
  - Does the builder record when it was discovered?
  - Is work stopped while approval is requested?
  - Is the additional labor/material cost calculated before calling?

  If nothing is captured before the call, the change begins as a verbal observation with no durable evidence.

  ### Customer phone call — approval is created

  Known:

  - The condition is explained verbally.
  - The customer says “okay.”
  - Work proceeds.

  We need to determine what “okay” means:

  - Okay, I understand the problem?
  - Okay, perform the work?
  - Okay, I accept the additional price?
  - Okay, I accept a price that will be calculated later?
  - Okay, I accept a schedule delay?

  Those are not the same approval.

  Questions:

  - Is a dollar amount stated during the call?
  - Is the exact added work described?
  - Is the schedule effect discussed?
  - Does the customer explicitly agree to pay?
  - Does anyone repeat the agreement back?
  - Is anyone else present to hear it?

  ### Exact death point

  Based on your current map, the semantic approval dies when the phone call ends.

  A call log may remain, but it proves only:

  > A call occurred between these numbers at this time.

  It does not prove:

  - What condition was discussed
  - What work was authorized
  - What price was accepted
  - Whether the customer agreed to pay
  - Whether a schedule change was accepted

  The approval may continue temporarily in human memory, but it no longer exists as a durable business record.

  Condition discovered
      ↓
  Phone call
      ↓
  Customer says “okay”
      ↓
  Call ends  ← durable approval dies here
      ↓
  Contractor relies on memory

  ### Work execution — approval is converted into cost

  After the call:

  - Builder performs extra labor.
  - Additional materials may be used.
  - Schedule may change.
  - Other work may be delayed.

  Questions:

  - Is extra labor tracked separately?
  - Are extra materials tied to the change?
  - Is the original problem photographed?
  - Is completion photographed?
  - Does the change receive a number or name?
  - Is the customer told when the extra work is complete?

  If not, the company cannot reliably calculate what the approved change actually cost.

  ### Invoice creation — approval must be reconstructed

  At invoicing, the owner must remember:

  - What happened
  - Why it was outside the original scope
  - What the customer approved
  - What it cost
  - What markup to apply
  - Whether it was already discussed
  - Whether the customer expects to see it

  This is the second major loss point.

  The owner may:

  - Forget to bill it
  - Estimate the charge from memory
  - Reduce it to avoid conflict
  - Include it without enough explanation
  - Bury it in the final total
  - Add it as a line item with no written approval
  - Decide the amount is not worth pursuing

  ### Customer receives the invoice — competing memories appear

  The customer may say:

  - “I approved the work, not that price.”
  - “You never said it would cost extra.”
  - “I thought it was included.”
  - “I never approved that amount.”
  - “You finished too quickly for it to cost that much.”
  - “I’ll pay the original estimate, but not the extra.”

  At this point, neither party has a controlling record. The dispute is decided by memory, leverage, relationship pressure, or willingness
  to pursue collection.

  ### Final outcome

  The change-order value can end as:

  - Fully paid
  - Partially paid
  - Negotiated downward
  - Rolled into the total without explanation
  - Forgotten
  - Written off
  - Disputed
  - Sent to collections
  - Taken to court
  - Absorbed by the contractor

  ## The complete failure chain

  Unexpected condition discovered
      ↓
  Little or no written evidence
      ↓
  Owner explains it by phone
      ↓
  Customer gives ambiguous verbal approval
      ↓
  Call ends; approval has no durable content
      ↓
  Extra work proceeds
      ↓
  Labor and materials are not isolated
      ↓
  Charge is reconstructed later from memory
      ↓
  Customer sees charge at invoicing
      ↓
  Customer’s memory conflicts with contractor’s memory
      ↓
  Contractor negotiates down, writes it off, or pursues a dispute
