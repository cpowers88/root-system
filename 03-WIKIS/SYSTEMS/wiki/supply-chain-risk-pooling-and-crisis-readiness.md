# Supply-Chain Risk, Pooling, and Crisis Readiness

**Summary**: Routine variability can be handled with standing inventory, capacity,
and time buffers. Rare consequential disruption requires a layered response:
pooling makes buffers more efficient, contingency plans prearrange virtual buffers
for anticipated scenarios, and crisis-management capability creates a response in
real time. Organizational readiness rests on flexibility, empowerment, awareness,
and communication; resilience is therefore a property of both the physical network
and the decision network.

## Match the Protection Layer to the Risk

| Risk condition | Primary response |
|---|---|
| Frequent, moderate variability | standing inventory, capacity, and time buffers |
| Several independent variability sources | pooled or flexible buffers |
| Identifiable high-impact scenario | contingency plan and contingent resources |
| Novel or rapidly changing event | crisis-management capability |

Large standing buffers are rarely economical for a low-probability six-month
shutdown. Conversely, inventing a response during an ordinary seasonal surge is a
failure to plan. Use probability, consequence, detection time, recovery time, and
customer criticality to choose the layer.

## Variability Pooling

Pooling combines independent or imperfectly correlated uncertainty behind a common
buffer. Means add directly, while independent variances add; therefore relative
variability and the safety allowance per unit decline as sources are combined.

Pooling can apply to:

- centralized or virtually shared inventory;
- generic components and postponement;
- flexible plants able to serve multiple markets;
- cross-trained labor and shared service capacity;
- product architectures with substitute components or alternate suppliers;
- chained flexibility, where a limited set of links approximates full flexibility.

Pooling loses value when risks are strongly positively correlated, the shared
resource itself becomes a single point of failure, or transportation/response time
negates the inventory reduction. Diversification that looks broad but depends on
one geography, tier-two supplier, technology, or logistics corridor is not true
pooling.

## Contingency Planning as Virtual Buffering

A contingency plan secures resources only if the triggering event occurs.

1. **Anticipate** the event class and threshold worth planning for.
2. **Design** roles, communication, alternate supply/capacity, priorities, and
   fallback stages.
3. **Prepare** contracts, data access, training, drills, and monitoring signals.
4. **Execute** when the trigger occurs.
5. **Update** as the network, risks, and post-event evidence change.

A named backup supplier is not a contingency resource until qualification,
capacity, commercial terms, tooling, data exchange, logistics, and activation
authority have been tested.

## Crisis Management

Crisis management differs mainly in sequence: the event is already unfolding when
the detailed response is designed. Preparation should therefore establish general
capabilities before the specific plan exists:

- a clear decision hierarchy and authority to act;
- monitoring and escalation channels;
- a cross-functional task-force pattern;
- accurate operational and dependency data;
- internal, supplier, customer, and public communication paths;
- routines for rapid experimentation and updating.

Execution is the ultimate test. Plans that are not known by substitutes, cannot be
activated outside office hours, or depend on inaccessible data are not operational.

## Four Dimensions of Readiness

- **Flexibility**: standardized work, compatible equipment, short cycle times,
  product substitutability, and alternate capacity allow resources to shift.
- **Empowerment**: people close to the event can make time-critical decisions within
  understood boundaries.
- **Awareness**: monitoring, weak-signal attention, dependency mapping, and drills
  detect trouble early.
- **Communication**: dense internal and interfirm links move facts and decisions
  faster than the disruption propagates.

The Toyota/Aisin fire response showed the value of a practiced supplier network:
partners divided designs and production assignments, began replacement deliveries
within two days, and restored Toyota plants within five despite the source plant's
much longer outage. Nokia's response to the Philips fire combined flexible product
design, alternate suppliers, monitoring, empowered troubleshooting, and rapid
communication; Ericsson's single-source dependence contributed to lost sales and
market share.

## Audit Sequence

1. Separate routine variability from low-frequency/high-consequence disruption.
2. Map shared dependencies beneath nominally diverse suppliers and facilities.
3. Test correlation before claiming pooling benefits.
4. Rank scenarios by consequence, likelihood, warning time, and recovery time.
5. Verify contingent resources through activation drills, not contracts alone.
6. Map decision authority, monitoring, and communication networks.
7. Run a loss-of-node exercise and observe who notices, decides, communicates, and
   restores service.
8. Feed incident and drill evidence back into product, supplier, inventory, and
   capacity design.

## Use / Retrieval Notes

**Use when**: Safety stock is the only resilience strategy, “backup” suppliers have
never been activated, or incident response depends on a few people and informal
contacts.

**Proof**: Routine variability has economical buffers, major scenarios have tested
contingent resources, and the organization demonstrates flexibility, empowered
decisions, early awareness, and rapid cross-boundary communication in a drill.
