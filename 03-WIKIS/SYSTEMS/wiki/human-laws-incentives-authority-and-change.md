# Human Laws: Incentives, Authority, and Sustainable Change

**Summary**: A production system consists of equipment, logic, and people. People
self-optimize, differ in capability and desired responsibility, can amplify a
change through advocacy, and burn out after repeated revolutions. Predictive plans
must use realistic evidence even when motivational targets are ambitious, and no
person should be held responsible for results they lack the authority, information,
capacity, or time to control.

## Four Human Laws

1. **Self-interest**: People, not organizations, self-optimize. Local behavior can
   be individually rational yet systemically harmful: sales overloads capacity,
   production maximizes long runs, repair stocks excessive parts, or operators
   cherry-pick favorable work.
2. **Individuality**: People differ in speed, skill, memory, motivation, and desired
   responsibility. A system that succeeds only because one exceptional person
   carries undocumented knowledge is not robust.
3. **Advocacy**: A champion can make a good or bad system appear successful. A
   durable change must survive the champion's departure.
4. **Burnout**: Repeated "revolutions" create rational cynicism. Workers learn that
   ignoring the latest program is often rewarded when it disappears.

Constraints are not automatically bad. Unlike a mathematical optimizer, an
organization does not reliably select the best point inside its feasible region.
WIP caps, design standards, coordinated sales rules, and quality gates can improve
behavior by preventing attractive local choices that damage the whole system.

## Design for Human Variation

- Match responsibility and control style to the actual workforce rather than an
  idealized operator.
- Cross-train and document tacit knowledge before a key person's absence exposes
  the dependency.
- Use nonfinancial motivation where appropriate; piece-rate incentives often
  destroy flow, quality, or priority discipline.
- Treat a system requiring a scheduling genius as a system defect, not proof of an
  excellent scheduler.

The bucket-brigade example shows that differences can be designed into the system:
placing workers slowest-to-fastest in a shared-task flow can naturally balance the
line. Equality of dignity does not imply identical operating characteristics.

## Sustainable Change

- Use transformation rhetoric sparingly.
- Explain why as well as how; teach the minimum system and variability concepts
  needed to operate the change intelligently.
- Pilot on a bounded line, product, or new operation before broad deployment.
- Test whether normal operators and managers can sustain the method without its
  founder.
- Institutionalize feedback, documentation, and ownership before rotating the
  champion away.

## Planning Is Not Motivation

Predictive tools - capacity plans, schedules, due-date quotes, staffing models -
must use the most accurate evidence available. Aspirational capacity can be useful
as a target, but inserting it into a promise system produces late orders and hides
the gap between current capability and desired improvement.

Maintain two explicit values where necessary:

- **planning value**: demonstrated capability used for commitments;
- **improvement target**: the higher value being pursued and tested.

The target replaces the planning value only after evidence shows the process can
reliably deliver it.

## Responsibility Requires Authority

Responsibility without commensurate authority is demoralizing and
counterproductive. Before assigning a measure, ask whether the person controls:

- incoming material and work availability;
- priority and sequence;
- quality of upstream inputs;
- staffing, maintenance, and capacity;
- customer commitments; and
- enough uninterrupted time to manage the operation.

Deming's red-bead experiment shows the danger of rewarding and punishing random
variation. Manufacturing and sales should have distinct accountability when sales
overcommits demand relative to agreed capacity. Managers responsible for real-time
flow also need protected operational attention, not merely nominal authority.

## Audit Questions

1. Which metric makes locally rational behavior harmful to the whole process?
2. Which operation depends on undocumented knowledge held by one person?
3. Would this program survive its champion leaving next month?
4. How many prior "revolutions" trained the workforce to wait this one out?
5. Are aspirational rates being used to promise customer dates?
6. Does each accountable owner control the inputs and decisions affecting the
   measure?
7. Does the operating manager actually have time to operate?

## Use / Retrieval Notes

**Use when**: A technically sound change stalls, metrics produce gaming, customer
commitments use wishful capacity, or accountability is assigned without control.

**Proof**: The changed system continues under ordinary staffing after the champion
steps back, and measures distinguish controllable performance from system noise.
