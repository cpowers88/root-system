# Commodity Cycles: Why Markets Don't Self-Correct, and How Price Discovery Actually Works

**Summary**: Markets add price-and-profit feedback on top of the supply-chain dynamics already covered — but this doesn't damp out the oscillation seen in earlier chapters; it adds new cycle periods on top of it. The chapter's generic commodity model distinguishes capacity from capacity utilization (two separate decisions on two very different time constants), and develops a robust, behaviorally-grounded price-setting formulation that explains both ordinary commodity price cycles and full-blown speculative bubbles as two ends of the same feedback structure.

**Sources**: BusinessDynamics.pdf (Sterman, *Business Dynamics: Systems Thinking and Modeling for a Complex World*, McGraw-Hill, 2000), Chapter 20 ("The Invisible Hand Sometimes Shakes: Commodity Cycles"), sections 20.1-20.2

## Commodity Cycles Are Endogenous, Not Just Demand Following the Business Cycle

Hogs cycle on roughly a 4-year period, cattle on 10-12 years, copper on 8-10 years (documented back to 1840), commercial aircraft on roughly 10 years — and critically, **these periods don't match each other or the ~3-5 year business cycle**, which is the chapter's core evidence that each commodity's cycle is generated by feedback structure *specific to that industry*, not simply inherited from aggregate economic fluctuation. **Cyclical instability isn't limited to literal undifferentiated commodities either** — the chapter explicitly extends the pattern to highly differentiated, capital-intensive industries (commercial aircraft, real estate, shipbuilding, semiconductors) and even to a pure service business with zero physical inventory (the century-long insurance underwriting cycle, already covered in [[business-cycle-origin-and-is-it-dead]]).

**Many industries generate two distinct, simultaneously-present cycle periods, not just one** — worth keeping as a standing expectation when diagnosing any client's industry-level cyclicality: a shorter 3-5 year cycle most visible in inventory, production, and price, layered on top of a much longer 10-20+ year cycle most visible in capacity (the oil tanker industry's price cycle and 20-year capacity cycle; the paper industry's 4-year and 10-15-year cycles; real estate's short-term pulse riding on its dominant 10-20-year cycle from [[real-estate-boom-bust-case-study]]). **The amplitude of the short-term cycle itself depends on the phase of the long cycle** — tanker prices are high *and* volatile when world fleet utilization is high, low and stable when there's excess capacity — meaning the two cycles aren't independent, they interact.

## Why the Classical "Cobweb Model" Is the Wrong Tool

The textbook economic explanation for commodity cycles (the cobweb model: demand responds to price immediately, supply responds with a lag) does capture the *core* idea — a time-delayed negative feedback in the supply response — but the chapter flags four specific, disqualifying flaws worth keeping as a standing critique of any oversimplified cobweb-style analysis a client or competing consultant might present: **(1)** it has no real stock-and-flow structure (no inventory, no WIP, no production capacity as a separate stock); **(2)** it's formulated in discrete time, which can generate spurious oscillatory artifacts purely from the choice of time step, unrelated to the real system; **(3)** it implicitly assumes the "period" of the model equals the production delay — but **real cycle periods are far longer than twice the production delay**: hog gestation/maturation is 11 months, yet the hog cycle is 4 years; commercial-building construction is ~3 years, yet the real estate cycle runs 10-20 years; **(4)** it conflates capacity with capacity *utilization*, so it can't explain why an industry shows two different cycle periods at once. **The general lesson, worth treating as a standing modeling discipline**: a proper commodity model has to represent the actual stock-flow structure, the actual delays (not stylized multiples of an arbitrary time unit), and the actual behavioral decision processes — exactly the same rigor demanded throughout this entire ingest.

## The Critical Distinction: Capacity vs. Capacity Utilization

This is the chapter's structural key to explaining the two-period phenomenon, and it's worth keeping as a clean, general principle for diagnosing any production-capacity question: **utilization decisions and capacity-investment decisions respond to the same underlying signal (profitability) but on completely different information and completely different time constants.**

- **Utilization** responds to the expected profitability of *already-existing* capacity — price vs. *variable* costs only, since fixed/capital costs are sunk and irrelevant to the decision to run an already-built plant a bit harder or softer. This adjusts comparatively fast (data-gathering, noise-filtering, and implementation delays, but no construction time).
- **Capacity** (new investment) responds to the expected profitability of *new* capacity — price vs. *total* costs (fixed plus variable), using a much longer-horizon price forecast, since new capacity has to remain profitable for years after it's built. This adjusts slowly, gated by the genuine multi-year capacity-acquisition delay.

**Why the aggregate utilization curve is smooth even though any single plant's run/don't-run decision is a sharp threshold**: real industries contain a distribution of plant efficiencies and a distribution of producer expectations — some operators are more optimistic or more efficient than others — so as the average markup ratio rises, an increasing *fraction* of the population crosses its own individual threshold, producing a smooth aggregate S-curve rather than a step function. **A directly transferable industry-classification insight**: capital-intensive industries (semiconductors, paper, chemicals) run at high, comparatively unresponsive utilization (operating near the flat top of the curve, since fixed costs dominate and idling capacity is expensive); industries with mostly variable costs (some agricultural commodities) run at lower average utilization and are far more sensitive to short-term price swings (operating on the steep middle of the curve).

## Desired Capacity: A Floating-Goal Hill-Climbing Process, Not an Optimization

**A pointed methodological point, directly invoking the "Baker Criterion" from elsewhere in the book**: many economic models assume producers can calculate the *optimal* long-run capacity level and then simply move toward it — but no one actually knows the true equilibrium capital stock for a commodity market, since it depends on deeply uncertain future demand growth, substitute development, and price elasticities. **The chapter's behaviorally-grounded alternative**: desired capacity is **anchored to current capacity, then adjusted up or down based on the expected profitability of new investment** — a genuine positive feedback (the "Capacity Goal Adjustment loop"): when new investment looks profitable, producers raise their aspiration *above* current capacity; once capacity catches up, and if profitability is still attractive, they raise the goal again. **This is explicitly a hill-climbing heuristic** (the same family of mechanism as Chapter 13's formulation principles) — capacity keeps growing as long as returns exceed normal, and keeps shrinking as long as they fall short, with no one ever needing to know the "correct" final answer. **The equilibrium that results is a genuine dynamic equilibrium**, not a static one — optimistic new entrants are continuously entering even when industry profits are merely normal, exactly balanced in aggregate by pessimistic incumbents exiting.

## Price Setting: Anchor-and-Adjust, With a Built-In Explanation for Both Ordinary Cycles and Speculative Bubbles

**Why the standard "price = fixed equilibrium price × demand/supply function" formulation is wrong, not just inconvenient**: a genuinely fixed equilibrium price can't represent a permanently changing cost environment (inflation, technology) without forcing the model into permanent, ever-widening disequilibrium — and assuming traders *know* the true equilibrium price violates the same Baker Criterion problem as the capacity case. **The chapter's fix, directly parallel to the capacity formulation**: price is anchored to **Traders' Expected Price** — a belief about the market-clearing price that itself adjusts gradually (first-order exponential smoothing) toward the *actual* price — then nudged away from that anchor by specific, well-evidenced cues: the inventory-coverage (demand/supply) imbalance, and the gap between expected price and expected production costs. **This produces a second hill-climbing/anchoring loop, structurally identical to the capacity-goal mechanism**: prices rise as long as the market is short, and as they rise, the *anchor itself* (trader beliefs about fair value) drifts upward to match — meaning the system can "discover" a market-clearing price without anyone needing to know the underlying supply/demand curves directly.

**The single most important behavioral result of this formulation, worth keeping as the chapter's central insight**: whether the system settles into an ordinary, bounded commodity-price cycle or runs away into a full speculative bubble is purely a question of **which feedback loop dominates**, determined by the *magnitude* of the inventory imbalance, not by any different underlying mechanism. For small/moderate imbalances, the negative Cost Pressure loop (price pulled back toward fundamental costs) dominates and price is "regressive" — it returns toward fundamental value. **For large enough imbalances, the positive Price Adjustment loop overwhelms the cost-anchoring effect entirely, and price rises exponentially without bound** until the underlying imbalance is resolved by some other channel (demand destruction, new supply) — **the same structure, the same formulation, just a shift in which loop dominates.** The 1970s silver bubble (700% in two years, then an even faster collapse) is the chapter's own example of this dominance shift running to its extreme. **The direct client-facing translation**: an "irrational" speculative bubble and an "ordinary" commodity cycle are not different phenomena requiring different explanations — they're the same price-discovery mechanism under different-sized shocks, and recognizing which regime a market is currently in (bounded cost-anchored cycling vs. runaway loop-dominance shift) is itself a meaningful diagnostic question.

**Two specific, instructive misformulation warnings worth flagging for any future modeling work**: capacity *utilization* and *delivery delay* are both **bad proxies for the demand/supply imbalance** in a price model, because both **saturate** — utilization can't exceed 100% and delivery delay can't fall below the minimum processing time, so neither variable can distinguish a 5% shortage from a 500% shortage even though price should clearly respond very differently to each. **Inventory coverage doesn't have this ceiling/floor problem**, which is why it's the chapter's preferred demand/supply indicator.

## Real-World Validation: This Isn't Just a Theoretical Formulation

The price-setting model has been applied with good results across genuinely disparate markets: Taylor (1999) on the paper industry, Homer (1996) on commodity chemicals, and — most strikingly — **Hines (1987) applied the identical structure to US bond-market interest rates from 1959-1980**, one of the most turbulent periods in that market's history, with "Traders' Expected Price" reinterpreted as bond traders' belief about the equilibrium interest rate and "inventory coverage" reinterpreted as the banking system's free reserves. The econometrically-estimated sensitivity (a 1% drop in free reserves → ~4% rate increase above the equilibrium expectation) and adjustment-time (1.4 years) closely matched independently-estimated inflation-expectation adjustment times — strong cross-validation that the same generic anchor-and-adjust price-discovery structure genuinely operates across markets as different as paper, chemicals, and government bonds.

## North Star Connection

- How this applies to the audit business: the capacity-vs-utilization distinction is a directly transferable diagnostic for any client weighing a "run harder" (utilization) decision against a "build more" (capacity) decision — these require fundamentally different cost data (variable-only vs. full cost) and different forecast horizons, and conflating them is a common, costly client error. The "is this an ordinary cycle or a loop-dominance-shift runaway" framing gives a precise, non-mystical way to discuss a client's pricing volatility without resorting to "the market is just crazy right now."
- Track relevance: Business / Systems — directly applicable to any client in a cyclical, capital-intensive, or commodity-adjacent industry (construction materials, certain subcontractor trades), and a strong general pricing/capacity-decision framework.
- Possible future Second Brain use: a "utilization vs. capacity decision" framing tool (which cost basis and time horizon actually applies to this specific decision) and a "regime check" (is this client's price volatility bounded/cost-anchored, or showing signs of loop-dominance runaway) are both strong candidate audit-diagnostic tools.
