# The Rise of the Modern Manufacturing Organization: Structure and the Human Element

**Summary**: How Du Pont and GM's Alfred Sloan invented the two structural templates every large manufacturing organization still uses (centralized-functional vs. decentralized-multidivisional), and how the Hawthorne studies and industrial-psychology pioneers exposed scientific management's blind spot on human motivation — plus the parallel rise of formal management education.

**Sources**: factoryPhysics.pdf (Hopp & Spearman, 3rd ed., Waveland Press), Chapter 1 ("Manufacturing in America"), sections 1.6–1.6.3

**Last updated**: 2026-06-21

---

## Two Structural Templates: Du Pont and GM

By the end of WWI, large vertically integrated organizations had fully exploited economies of *scale* for a single product. Further growth required economies of *scope* — sharing production and distribution resources across multiple products — which demanded new organizational structures.

**Pierre Du Pont** consolidated a collection of single-function explosives makers into a centrally governed, multidepartmental, integrated organization by 1917, installing Taylor's control techniques, psychological testing for personnel selection, and — his most influential innovation — **return on investment (ROI)** as the standard for comparing department performance. This produced the **centralized, functional department structure** — still the default for firms with a single product line in a single market.

**Alfred Sloan**, brought in by Du Pont (who had become General Motors' largest investor after bailing out founder William Durant) to fix a company that had grown by acquisition into "an empire without structure," designed the opposite approach: a collection of **autonomous operating divisions** (Cadillac/high price, Chevrolet/low price to compete directly with Ford, Buick and Oldsmobile in the middle, Pontiac added mid-1920s) coordinated — but not run — by a strong general office. Sloan's goal: **"a car for every purse and purpose."** GM borrowed Du Pont's ROI methods for evaluating divisions and added demand forecasting, inventory tracking, and market-share estimation — procedures that are still standard, in modified form, across American industry today.

**This produced the decentralized, multidivisional structure** — the default for firms with multiple product lines or markets. The results were decisive: GM went from a distant second (12.3% market share in 1921 vs. Ford's 55.7%) to 32.3% by 1929 while Ford fell to 31.3% (and eventually to 18.9% by 1940, third place behind Chrysler). **Ford's failure to adopt a managerial hierarchy — Henry Ford's stated desire to "manage without managers" — is the direct cause Peter Drucker assigns to its precipitous decline** (see [[american-manufacturing-origins-and-system]]).

**Audit-usable framing**: a client business that has outgrown a single-line/single-market structure but is still organized as if it were one (i.e., still run as a single centralized hierarchy despite multiple distinct product or service lines) is showing a GM-1908-style structural mismatch — a Sloan-style divisional reorganization, even informally, may be the actual fix rather than a process tweak.

## Hawthorne and the Human Element

Scientific management's primary goals — motivating workers and matching them to tasks — were behavioral, but Taylor treated human beings as if they could be optimized like a metal-cutting machine (e.g., reasoning that since a worker "strains every nerve" in a baseball game, similar exertion should be achievable at work). He showed no appreciation for the psychological difference between work and play, and never managed to understand the worker's point of view despite a stated concern for them.

This blind spot was filled by a parallel track of **industrial psychology** pioneers:

- **Hugo Munsterberg** (acknowledged father of industrial psychology, Harvard) — his 1913 *Psychology and Industrial Efficiency* organized the field around "The Best Possible Man" (selection), "The Best Possible Work" (training/conditions), and "The Best Possible Effect" (achieving management goals).
- **Walter Dill Scott** — studied worker selection/promotion, raised manager awareness of psychology through a popular 1910–1911 magazine series.
- **Lillian Gilbreth** — added psychology to her husband Frank's motion-study work; her 1914 *The Psychology of Management* argued scientific management's emphasis on selection, training, and functional foremanship actually offered *more* room for individual development than traditional centralized management — an early, if apologetic, call for humanizing management.
- **Mary Parker Follett** — see [[scientific-management-and-taylor]]; argued for participation, not just consent, as the basis of social/work relations.

**The Hawthorne studies** (Western Electric, Chicago, 1924–1932) began as a simple question — does illumination affect productivity? — and got an unexpected answer: productivity rose when light increased, but *also* rose when it was decreased to moonlight levels. Subsequent tests of rest periods, work-week length, incentive plans, free lunches, and supervisory style all produced higher-than-normal output. **George Elton Mayo's** interpretation, reduced in popular legend to "the Hawthorne effect" (productivity rises simply because workers are being watched/studied), was actually more nuanced in his own writing: work is fundamentally a *group activity*, and workers strive for a sense of belonging, not just financial gain. This shifted management's emphasis from Taylorist technical efficiency toward a richer **human relations** orientation — listening and counseling as managerial tools, not just job design and pay.

**Audit-usable framing**: the Hawthorne effect is a genuine measurement-validity caution worth applying to any client-facing improvement initiative — if you're observing and measuring a process closely as part of an audit, expect some performance lift purely from the attention, independent of whatever fix you eventually recommend. Don't mistake that lift for proof the fix worked.

## Management Education

Before 1920, large businesses were mostly run by owner-entrepreneurs (Carnegie, Ford, Du Pont). Post-WWI growth outpaced what owners could personally manage, creating real demand for a **professional managerial class** drawn from outside the ownership structure. Formal training followed two parallel tracks: business schools (Wharton 1881, then Chicago, Berkeley, Dartmouth's first master's program, NYU, Wisconsin by 1900 — Harvard became the leading program, relying heavily on outside scientific-management lecturers like Taylor, Emerson, and Barth, and pioneering the case method) and industrial engineering departments (Hugo Diemer's first shop-management course, University of Kansas 1901–02; first full IE curriculum, Penn State 1908). Both tracks were heavily shaped by scientific management at their founding.

## Key Takeaways

- Every large manufacturing organization still runs on one of two structural templates invented in the 1910s–20s: Du Pont's centralized-functional model (single product/market) or Sloan's decentralized-multidivisional model (multiple products/markets) — a fast structural-fit question for any client whose org chart doesn't match its actual product/market diversity.
- The Hawthorne effect is a measurement-validity caution, not just a historical curiosity — expect observation itself to produce a temporary performance lift independent of any actual intervention.
- The professional-manager class and the scientific-management-influenced business school both emerged from the same post-WWI scale problem: owners could no longer personally run what they'd built.
