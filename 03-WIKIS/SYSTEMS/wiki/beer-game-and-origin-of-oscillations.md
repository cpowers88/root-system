# The Beer Distribution Game: Why Ignoring the Supply Line Causes Supply Chain Oscillation

**Summary**: The famous role-play simulation where ordinary people, business executives, and senior government officials alike generate massive, costly oscillation in a simple four-stage supply chain — not because of any external randomness (customer demand barely changes), but because nearly everyone systematically ignores the "supply line" of orders already placed but not yet received. A precise mathematical formula (the Weight on the Supply Line) explains exactly why, and the same mistake is shown to be a universal feature of everyday human behavior, not a business-specific failure.

**Sources**: BusinessDynamics.pdf (Sterman, *Business Dynamics: Systems Thinking and Modeling for a Complex World*, McGraw-Hill, 2000), Chapter 17 ("Supply Chains and the Origin of Oscillations"), sections 17.4.1-17.4.2

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

## A Delay Alone Doesn't Cause Oscillation — Mismanaging It Does

A crucial, non-obvious distinction the chapter establishes before the Beer Game case: **the mere existence of a time delay between ordering and receiving does not, by itself, produce oscillation.** [[stock-management-structure-and-amplification]]'s capital-investment example has a real 1.5-year acquisition delay and still settles smoothly to a new equilibrium with no oscillation at all — *because* the model assumes managers track the supply line and reduce new orders once enough is already "in the pipeline" to close the gap. **Oscillation specifically requires the delay to be (at least partially) ignored** — the decision-maker must keep initiating corrective actions in response to a perceived gap *even after* sufficient correction is already in transit and will arrive soon.

## The Game Itself

Forrester's late-1950s role-play (still played worldwide today, by audiences ranging from high schoolers to CEOs and senior government officials): four players — Retailer, Wholesaler, Distributor, Factory — each manage one stage of a beer supply chain using physical chips and order slips, trying to minimize their own combined inventory-holding and stockout costs. **The test condition is almost insultingly simple**: customer orders (drawn from a deck of cards) step up just once, from 4 to 8 cases/week, and otherwise stay completely constant for the rest of the game. There is no randomness, no breakdowns, no capacity constraints, and the full structure is visible to every player on the board.

**The result, replicated across decades of play**: average costs run roughly **10 times** the theoretical optimum on a first play. Every link generates the same qualitative pattern — inventories first decline (often into backlog) as the step hits, triggering a growing wave of orders that amplifies at each stage upstream; eventually factory production surges and overcorrects, sending inventory soaring well past the target; players then slash orders (often to literal zero for extended stretches); the resulting glut only slowly drains. **The cycle period runs 20-25 weeks; factory production amplifies customer orders by roughly a factor of 4 on average, peaking some 15 weeks after the single demand step** — oscillation, amplification, and phase lag, the same three signatures identified in real industrial data, generated here by a near-trivial, fully transparent, fully deterministic system. **The single most important fact about the game, restated for emphasis**: customer demand is essentially flat throughout — the wild swings are entirely **endogenous**, generated by how the players themselves manage their own inventories, not imposed on them from outside.

## The Mechanism, Made Mechanically Obvious by a Worked Table

The source walks through a discrete week-by-week simulation (Table 17-2) of a manager who **completely ignores the supply line** (orders react only to the current inventory gap): an unexpected 100-unit inventory loss triggers an order for 200 (100 to replace customer orders, 100 to close the gap) — but because of a 3-week delivery delay, the *next* week's inventory report still shows the same shortfall (the first order hasn't arrived yet), so the manager orders 200 *again*, and again, and again, for five straight weeks, building a massive 600-unit supply line before the first delivery even lands. Once deliveries start arriving, inventory swings wildly past the target (peaking near 1200 units) before the manager — now drowning in excess stock — slashes orders to zero, which (because the existing supply line still has to drain) only makes the eventual undershoot worse. **The system settles into a sustained limit cycle, never converging, purely from this one behavioral flaw, with the realistic parameters chosen.**

**The exact same scenario, run with the manager giving the supply line equal weight to inventory on hand**, reaches equilibrium cleanly in just 4 weeks with **zero oscillation** — same delay, same shock, same desired inventory level, the *only* difference is whether the decision rule accounts for what's already in the pipeline.

## The Precise Formula: Weight on the Supply Line (WSL)

Sterman derives a clean closed-form result by combining the inventory-adjustment and supply-line-adjustment terms into a single **effective total stock** (ETS = inventory + WSL × supply line, where WSL = Stock Adjustment Time / Supply Line Adjustment Time). **The mathematical punchline**: when WSL = 1 (full weight on the supply line), the entire ordering system collapses back into an ordinary first-order negative feedback loop on ETS — and **first-order systems, per [[multiple-loop-systems-and-loop-dominance]], cannot oscillate, period.** When WSL = 0 (the supply line is completely ignored), the delay's effect is left fully unmanaged, and oscillation results. **The closer WSL is to 1, the more damped and stable the system becomes** — WSL is, in effect, a single dial that runs continuously from "guaranteed stable" to "guaranteed unstable," and it depends entirely on a decision-maker's mental model, not on anything about the physical supply chain itself.

**Estimating this rule against 44 real Beer Game players (Sterman 1989b) found it explained 71% of the variance in their actual order decisions** — a strong fit for a model using only three cues (incoming orders, inventory, supply line) — and the estimated average WSL was just **0.34**; a full third of subjects had an estimated WSL statistically indistinguishable from zero; only 25% gave the supply line more than half its proper weight. **A striking confirmation of how systematic (not random) this error is**: Sterman simulated one of the four "games" shown in his results using only the average estimated parameters from real players plus a small noise term — readers (including experienced practitioners) generally cannot tell the simulated game apart from a real one. **The error is robust even when the supply line is made maximally visible**: in several follow-up experiments (Diehl and Sterman 1995; Brehmer 1992) the supply line was prominently displayed on screen, and subjects still systematically underweighted it — confirming, per [[barriers-to-learning-and-virtual-worlds]], that the problem is in the *mental model* used to interpret available information, not in the information's availability.

## Why Almost Everyone Makes This Mistake — A Developmental and Incentive Explanation

The chapter offers a genuinely compassionate explanation rather than treating this as simple stupidity. **It's developmental**: infants experience the world as immediate cause-and-effect (cry → fed), and learning to recognize and patiently wait out a delay — to actually trust that "coming right up" means the juice is already in the supply line — is a slow maturational skill, not an innate one. **A set of universal, instantly recognizable everyday examples make the point vivid and memorable**: overheating a stove burner and burning dinner because the heating coil keeps radiating heat after being turned down; repeatedly clicking an unresponsive web page and ending up somewhere unintended once the stacked clicks all fire at once; scalding yourself in an unfamiliar hotel shower by over-correcting the temperature before the pipes have caught up; tailgating and braking too late on the highway because of reaction-time and momentum lag; drinking too much too fast and only feeling the full effect once the alcohol already in your stomach has fully absorbed (the chapter explicitly notes this exact mechanism kills young people via alcohol poisoning every year).

**Why these everyday failures persist into adulthood despite the obvious cost**: the learning conditions in genuinely high-stakes business/economic systems are much *worse* than in the shower or the car. Albert Aftalion's early-1900s fireplace analogy is quoted directly as a pre-system-dynamics description of exactly this mechanism applied to business cycles: continuing to add coal because the room still feels cold, without accounting for the coal already glowing in the grate, "fatally" overheats the room once it all ignites — and Aftalion argued individual entrepreneurs do the same thing with capital investment, focusing only on today's profitability while ignoring the capacity already under construction across the whole industry. **Why market discipline doesn't reliably weed this behavior out**: it's a problem of *aggregation*, not just individual ignorance — an individual firm typically does track its own supply line of materials or capital it's directly ordered, but treats the *industry-wide* supply line (everyone else's capacity under construction) as exogenous, since no single firm has visibility into or responsibility for the whole market's pipeline. When every firm reacts only to today's high profits while ignoring everyone else doing the same thing simultaneously, the result is collective overshoot regardless of how rational each individual firm's own internal accounting is.

**The LTCM case is the chapter's sharpest illustration that sophistication doesn't immunize against this failure**: Long Term Capital Management, staffed by PhD-level quants using advanced mathematical models, collapsed in 1998 in significant part because their models didn't treat other hedge funds' simultaneous, correlated unwinding as an input — "that wasn't an input to anybody's model," in one trader's own words, quoted directly. **The same open-loop, treat-the-aggregate-as-exogenous mistake that ordinary Beer Game players make with a four-person supply chain recurred, at vastly larger scale and with vastly more financial sophistication, in one of the most consequential market failures of the era.**

## North Star Connection

- How this applies to the audit business: the WSL diagnostic ("does this client's ordering/staffing/hiring decision explicitly account for what's already in the pipeline, or only react to the current gap?") is a fast, structurally precise question that can immediately explain a client's recurring boom-bust ordering pattern without requiring a full formal model. The everyday-analogy list (the burner, the shower, the highway, the drink) is a uniquely effective, instantly relatable way to make this abstract systems concept land with a non-technical client audience.

## Use / Retrieval Notes

**Best use**:
Client discovery and explanation tool — ask whether a client's ordering/hiring/investment decisions explicitly track what's already "in the pipeline" or only react to the current visible gap, and use the everyday-analogy list (burner, web clicks, shower, highway, drinking) to make the underlying concept land quickly with a non-technical audience.

**Use when**:
A client describes a recurring boom-bust pattern in ordering, staffing, or inventory that seems disproportionate to actual changes in their end demand.

**Do not use when**:
The client genuinely has no meaningful delay between initiating an order/hire/investment and its effect — the supply-line mechanism specifically requires a real lag to apply.
