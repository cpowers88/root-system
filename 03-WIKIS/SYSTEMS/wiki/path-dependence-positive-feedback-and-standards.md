# Path Dependence, Positive Feedback, and Standards

**Summary**: In a path-dependent system, small early events are amplified by
reinforcing feedback and determine which otherwise plausible equilibrium emerges.
As adoption and complementary investment accumulate, switching becomes harder
and the system can lock into a standard, capability, supplier, technology, or way
of working.

## Path Dependence

Negative feedback pulls a system toward a stable attractor; modest differences in
initial conditions disappear. Positive feedback pushes a system away from an
unstable balance. Small random events or external perturbations are amplified, so
microscopic early differences can produce macroscopically different outcomes.

Path dependence has three signatures:

1. Several outcomes are plausible at the beginning.
2. Early sequence and timing materially affect which outcome emerges.
3. The cost of changing paths rises as the system accumulates users, assets,
   expectations, routines, and complements.

Lock-in is therefore a dynamic condition, not merely resistance to change.

## The Polya Process

The Polya urn provides the minimal model. A new item is more likely to take the
type already common in the population. Each addition therefore reinforces its own
future probability. Early random draws have a large effect; later additions have
less influence as the installed population grows.

The system eventually stabilizes, but the equilibrium proportion depends on its
history. Accumulation converts an initially easy-to-influence choice into a state
that is costly or improbable to reverse.

## Why Standards Lock In

A standard becomes more attractive as more people use it:

- users learn the dominant format and reduce their personal switching cost;
- producers invest in compatible products and infrastructure;
- complementary goods become more available;
- service, support, and labor markets deepen;
- expectations of future adoption reduce perceived risk;
- firms optimize their operations around the installed base.

The VHS/Betamax case shows that technical merit alone does not determine the
winner. Recording time, manufacturing alliances, distribution, complementary
content, and installed-base momentum interacted. The unit of analysis is the
whole ecosystem, not the isolated product specification.

## Reinforcing Loops Behind Corporate Growth

| Loop family | Reinforcing mechanism |
|---|---|
| Awareness and installed base | More customers create visibility, references, and word of mouth |
| Scale and learning | Volume lowers unit development/production cost and improves capability |
| Price-cost position | Lower cost permits attractive pricing, increasing volume |
| Network effects | Each user increases value for other users |
| Complementary goods | A larger base attracts complements, which make the core offer more useful |
| Differentiation and innovation | Resources fund development, improving attractiveness and further growth |
| Market power | Scale improves channel access, bargaining power, and influence over standards |
| Mergers and acquisitions | Resources enable acquisition of capabilities, customers, and distribution |
| Workforce quality and loyalty | Success attracts and retains talent, strengthening execution |
| Cost of capital | Performance and confidence lower financing cost and expand access to capital |
| Rules of the game | Dominant firms can shape conventions, interfaces, and policy in their favor |
| Ambition and aspirations | Success raises goals and willingness to invest, reinforcing growth |

Durable advantage usually comes from a mutually consistent ensemble of loops.
One isolated loop is easier for competitors to copy or neutralize.

## Does Lock-In Select an Inferior Technology?

Path dependence makes inferior lock-in possible, but does not prove that every
dominant standard is inferior. Claims must define inferior for whom, under what
criteria, at what time, and including which switching and ecosystem costs.

The current state may remain efficient after complementary investment is counted,
even if another design would have been preferable before lock-in. Conversely, a
dominant design can persist despite poor present performance because transition
costs and coordination risk are distributed across many actors.

## Limits to Lock-In

Lock-in can be broken by:

- an architectural shift that makes the old complement base less relevant;
- a major external shock or regulation;
- compatibility or translation layers that reduce switching cost;
- a sufficiently large performance advantage;
- coordinated migration, education, and infrastructure change;
- deliberate seeding of users and complements before the incumbent responds.

Success in one regime can create rigidity. Assets, metrics, beliefs, and skills
optimized for the old path can prevent the incumbent from responding to a new
architecture.

## Standards-Formation Strategy

Before the market locks:

1. Seed enough users to create visible momentum.
2. Seed complementary goods as well as the core product.
3. Form alliances or joint ventures that increase ecosystem credibility.
4. Reduce adoption and switching cost through compatibility and migration support.
5. Target influential users or channels whose adoption changes others' expectations.
6. Test policies against competitor response and the full feedback structure.

After lock-in, small promotional changes are unlikely to move the system. The
intervention must weaken incumbent reinforcing loops, lower coordination costs, or
create a new architecture with a different basis of competition.

## Audit Translation

When a client says "we have always done it this way," inventory the accumulated
reinforcement:

- installed tools, data formats, contracts, training, and integrations;
- customer and supplier expectations;
- complementary products and specialized labor;
- metrics, incentives, and capital tied to the current path;
- switching costs borne by different actors;
- early choices or accidents that became treated as necessities.

This distinguishes a justified operating standard from path-dependent inertia.
