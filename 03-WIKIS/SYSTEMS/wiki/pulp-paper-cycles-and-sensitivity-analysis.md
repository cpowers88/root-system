# Two Cycles, One Model: The Pulp and Paper Industry, and How to Stress-Test Any Model Before Trusting It

**Summary**: Applying the generic commodity model from [[commodity-cycles-and-the-generic-market-model]] to the pulp and paper industry — with zero structural changes, only different parameters — reproduces both of the industry's real, simultaneously-present cycle periods (a ~4-year inventory/price cycle and a ~14-year capacity cycle) from a single demand shock, matching the real data's relative amplitudes and phase relationships closely. Closes with the chapter's rigorous discipline for sensitivity analysis — both parametric and structural — that should be applied before trusting any model's conclusions. This closes out Chapter 20 and the approved Parts I/II/V ingest scope of Business Dynamics.

## The Real Data: Three Distinct Periodicities in One Industry

Real pulp and paper industry data (Taylor 1999) shows: a **3-5 year cycle** dominant in price (swinging ±40% around its long-run trend), inventory coverage (±20%), and capacity utilization (a comparatively narrow 85-95% band); a longer **10-15 year (Kuznets/construction) cycle**, most visible in capacity itself; and a much longer **~60-year economic-long-wave** pattern (capacity/production rising relative to trend from the late 1940s through 1973, then declining relative to trend afterward). **The chapter's own framing of why this matters for model credibility**: a model that can only reproduce *one* of these periods isn't actually capturing the industry's real structure — it's just fitting a single curve.

## Applying the Generic Model With Zero Structural Changes

**The headline methodological result, worth keeping as the single sharpest takeaway from this case**: the pulp and paper industry required **no new feedback loops, no new stocks, no structural modification whatsoever** to the generic commodity model from [[commodity-cycles-and-the-generic-market-model]] — only realistic parameter values specific to the industry (a 4-year capacity acquisition delay including site selection, financing, permitting, and construction; a 20-year average plant lifetime; ~60% fixed costs driving a normal 90% utilization target; a demand elasticity around -0.5 with a 6-month adjustment delay, per Taylor's own econometric estimate). **This is strong, direct evidence for the generic model's actual generality** — the same underlying structure, just re-parameterized, rather than a bespoke model built fresh for each industry.

**The simulation result, from a single clean test (a one-time 25% pulse in customer orders)**: the model spontaneously produces **two distinct oscillatory modes simultaneously** — inventory, price, utilization, and production cycling with a period just under **4 years** (generated by the delays in the Capacity Utilization loop), and capacity itself cycling with a period of about **14 years** (generated by the much longer delays in the Capacity Acquisition loop). **A specific, sharp phase-lag detail worth keeping**: price isn't simply the mirror image of inventory coverage — price *continues rising* for a while even after inventory coverage has already started to recover, both because of the (short) delay in perceiving coverage changes and, more importantly, because of the price-discovery process itself (the anchor/trader-belief mechanism from [[commodity-cycles-and-the-generic-market-model]] continues pulling price upward as long as coverage remains below normal, even past the point where coverage has begun to turn).

**Adding realistic random demand noise** (5% standard deviation, 1-year autocorrelation) instead of a single clean pulse produces irregular but recognizable versions of both cycles, with **relative amplitudes matching the real data closely**: capacity and utilization vary least (utilization mostly 85-95%, capacity roughly ±10%); inventory and price vary much more (roughly 60-150% of equilibrium); and the **capacity acquisition rate — the model's proxy for capital expenditure — shows the single largest swing of all (roughly 30-200% of equilibrium)**, a direct, quantified instance of the amplification-compounds-at-each-additional-stock principle from [[manufacturing-supply-chain-model]] and [[supply-chain-interactions-and-trust]], now showing up specifically as capital spending volatility. **The honest caveat the chapter itself states**: the simulated industry is *smoother* than the real data, because only one source of randomness (demand) was modeled — in reality, utilization, costs, investment, and other variables are all independently noisy, and the parameters here were mostly round numbers rather than econometrically fit. **The explicit, important point this caveat supports**: the model's value isn't a perfect numerical fit — it's correctly reproducing the *phase relationships and relative amplitudes* of two genuinely distinct cyclical modes from one parsimonious structure.

## The Chapter's Closing Discipline: Sensitivity Analysis Is Not Optional

**Three distinct reasons to run sensitivity analysis, worth keeping as a standing checklist for any model before presenting it to a client**: (1) it builds your own intuition about the structure-behavior relationship; (2) it lets you and the client test whether your conclusions are robust to the genuine uncertainty in your parameter estimates; (3) it actively **guides where to spend further data-collection effort** — a parameter that strongly moves the result is worth refining further; a parameter the result barely depends on isn't, even if it currently feels uncertain. **A pointed, money-saving warning worth keeping**: "modelers often spend too much time refining estimates for parameters that simply do not matter" — sensitivity testing tells you in advance which refinements are actually worth the cost.

**The specific testing protocol, worth adopting as a standard practice for any future model work**: write down your *prediction* for each parameter test *before* running it, including your reasoning — then compare the actual result to your prediction and revise your understanding of the mechanism if you were wrong. **This deliberate predict-then-test loop is explicitly framed as the fastest way to build genuine intuition about dynamics**, not just a box-checking formality. **How aggressively to vary a parameter**: because people (including modelers) are systematically overconfident about their own uncertainty (directly the same overconfidence research from [[first-order-systems-growth-decay-and-doubling-time]]), vary parameters by *more* than your stated uncertainty range suggests — if you think a parameter is uncertain by ±20%, test ±50% or more. **A second, complementary technique**: try genuinely extreme changes, including zeroing out an effect entirely to see what feedback loop disappears and how much that loop actually mattered.

**A critical limitation of one-at-a-time (univariate) testing, worth flagging explicitly before relying on it alone**: because real systems (and realistic models of them) are highly nonlinear, **the combined effect of multiple simultaneous parameter changes is generally not the sum of their individual effects**, and a 50% change in a parameter doesn't necessarily have twice the impact of a 25% change. Univariate sensitivity testing is a useful, necessary starting point, but a complete robustness check requires multivariate testing as well (left to the book's subsequent, out-of-scope Chapter 21 for full treatment).

## Beyond Parameters: Structural Sensitivity Is a Separate, Equally Necessary Check

**The chapter's final, important distinction**: testing your model's sensitivity to *parameter* uncertainty is necessary but not sufficient — every model also omits some feedback processes, aggregates some distinct actors together, and assumes some sources/sinks have infinite capacity (directly [[aggregation-and-challenging-the-clouds]]'s "challenge the clouds" discipline, now framed as a formal robustness test rather than just a scoping question). **The chapter's worked illustration of what a skeptical client team will actually ask**: "you've omitted a feedback we think matters" — and the proper response isn't to argue from authority, it's to **add the hypothesized structure, predict its effect in advance, then test it** exactly like a parameter sensitivity run. The chapter's own named examples (left as challenges, but instructive as a checklist of *what kinds* of structural challenges to expect from a sophisticated client): variable capacity lifetime (can producers decommission early or extend old plants' life based on profitability — would this dampen or worsen the capacity cycle?); order cancellation in the capacity supply line (the same vacancy-cancellation logic from [[labor-supply-chain-and-overtime-stabilization]], applied to capital orders); and **extrapolative rather than purely adaptive price expectations** — directly modeling the client's own qualitative claim ("during booms, a kind of euphoria spreads... this leads to overinvestment and worsens the next slump") using the TREND function, converting an anecdotal client observation into a testable structural hypothesis.
