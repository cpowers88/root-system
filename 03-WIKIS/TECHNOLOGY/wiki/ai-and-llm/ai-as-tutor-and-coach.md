# AI as a Tutor and Coach

**Summary**: How AI changes skill-building — for students (Ch. 7, "AI as a Tutor") and for professionals practicing a craft (Ch. 8, "AI as a Coach"). Both chapters converge on the same paradox: foundational knowledge and deliberate practice matter *more*, not less, once AI is available, even as AI also acts as a powerful skill-leveler.

**Sources**: CoIntelligence.pdf (Ch. 7-8)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-17

---

## Prompt engineering is a temporary skill

Mollick is explicit that today's careful prompting tricks — chain-of-thought prompting, the "take a deep breath" anecdote (telling a model to slow down measurably improves some outputs) — are workarounds for present-day model limitations, not durable expertise. As models improve, most of this syntax-level skill evaporates. What doesn't evaporate is understanding *what* to ask for and *how to judge* what comes back — the same judgment a good delegator needs with any junior employee (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

## Flipped classrooms and AI tutors

Two concrete cases anchor Ch. 7's classroom argument: Benjamin Breen's Black Death historical simulator (students interrogate an AI playing historical roles) and Khan Academy's Khanmigo (a personalized AI tutor layered onto existing curriculum). Both substitute AI-mediated, personalized instruction for some of what a flipped classroom tries to do with video lectures and class-time practice. Mollick frames AI tutoring as a genuine lever for narrowing the *global* education gap — students without access to expensive one-on-one tutoring can get a reasonable substitute (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

## The post-school training gap

Ch. 8 opens with a structural problem: traditional professional training relies on an apprenticeship ladder — junior people learn by doing the simple, repetitive version of a job under supervision (the chapter's example is surgical robots changing how trainee surgeons build hand skills, and the broader phenomenon of "shadow learning," where juniors absorb expertise informally just by being in the room). If AI absorbs the entry-level tasks that used to teach those skills, the ladder's bottom rungs disappear — and with them, the informal mechanism that used to produce tomorrow's experts (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

## The paradox: foundational practice matters more, not less

Mollick's answer to the training-gap problem is not "skip the fundamentals because AI can do them" — it's the opposite. Two analogies carry this argument:

- **Sophie/Naomi (piano)** — a student who lets the AI play for her never builds the ear and muscle memory that make her able to *evaluate* good playing; a student who uses the AI as a practice partner while still doing the work builds real skill faster.
- **Alex/Raj (architecture)** — the same split applied to a design discipline: using AI to skip the work of learning to see proportion and structure produces someone who can't tell a good design from a bad one, even with AI's help.

The throughline: AI is most valuable to people who already have enough foundational expertise to judge its output critically. Without that base, you can't tell when the AI is right, generic, or wrong — which is the same caution embedded in [[four-rules-for-co-intelligence]]'s "always invite AI to the table, but verify everything" stance (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

Wharton's AI-based pitch-practice simulator is offered as a working example of deliberate practice *with* an AI coach rather than practice *replaced by* AI.

## When everyone is an expert: AI as a skill-leveler

Even as it raises the bar for what counts as foundational mastery, AI compresses the *gap* between strong and weak performers — the "10x engineer" effect shrinks because AI assistance disproportionately helps the people who previously struggled most:

- BCG's internal study found the performance gap between top and bottom performers on a task shrank from 22% to 4% when both groups used AI (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).
- Law students' writing quality became more equal with AI assistance than without it.
- The Turnitin CEO's anecdote: hiring directly from high school becomes more viable when AI assistance flattens some of the experience gap a college degree used to certify.

Mollick speculates that skill at working with AI itself — not prompting syntax, but judgment about when and how to delegate, verify, and integrate AI output — is becoming a new, durable form of expertise, illustrated through his and his wife Dr. Lilach Mollick's own experience co-developing AI-assisted teaching methods.
