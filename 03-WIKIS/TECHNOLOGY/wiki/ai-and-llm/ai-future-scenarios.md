# AI Future Scenarios

**Summary**: Mollick's four-scenario framework (Ch. 9, "AI as Our Future") for where AI capability goes next, plus the closing argument — drawn from Tolkien's "eucatastrophe" — that the right response is to deliberately aim for the good outcome rather than passively risk the bad one. Closes out with the book's Epilogue, "AI as Us."

**Sources**: CoIntelligence.pdf (Ch. 9, Epilogue)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-17

---

## The four scenarios

Mollick lays out four explicit possibilities for AI's near-term capability trajectory, each with different stakes and a different posture toward action:

**Scenario 1 — As Good as It Gets.** AI capability plateaus roughly where it is now. Mollick argues this is technically unlikely, but still worth exploring because of its consequences even at current capability: undetectable deepfakes, a more fragmented information environment (filter bubbles), more engaging/addictive AI companions as companies optimize for engagement, and continued disruption to work purely through automating existing tasks — no further capability gains required (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

**Scenario 2 — Slow Growth.** Capability improves roughly 10-20% per year, which Mollick likens to the incremental improvement of television technology over decades rather than a sudden leap. This is the most "manageable" scenario — disruption arrives gradually enough that institutions and individuals have time to adapt. He flags two specific upsides: faster scientific discovery (a side discussion on the "burden of knowledge" — research productivity has been slowing roughly 50% every 13 years as the body of prior knowledge to master keeps growing — and AI's potential to help reverse that) and more educational opportunity for people without existing access (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

**Scenario 3 — Exponential Growth.** Capability keeps doubling Moore's-Law-style, potentially accelerated by an AI flywheel where AI systems help build the next generation of AI. Risks become both more severe and harder to predict: every computer system vulnerable to AI-assisted hacking, AI-generated influence campaigns at scale, and a citation of a real pre-LLM case where a drug-discovery AI tool was repurposed in six hours to invent VX nerve gas and worse — illustrating that the dual-use risk isn't hypothetical. Containment in this scenario is itself uncomfortable: "good" AI is used to filter dangerous or misleading content, which Mollick explicitly flags as carrying an Orwellian tinge, and governments may use AI surveillance to suppress AI-enabled crime and terrorism in ways that also expand state control over citizens ("AI-tocracy"). Upsides are also larger: AI companions becoming compelling enough to meaningfully reduce loneliness (with a new, different form of social isolation emerging alongside it), AI unlocking individual human potential (entrepreneurship and research accelerated by AI as both inspiration and labor-saving tool), and AI-powered robots/autonomous agents potentially reducing the need for human work enough to require real policy adaptation — shortened workweeks, UBI. Mollick grounds this with a historical analogy: average lifetime work hours have already fallen sharply since 1865 (from 124,000 hours to 69,000 in Britain by 1980; U.S. share of life spent working fell from 50% to 20%) without social collapse, suggesting humans can absorb large reductions in required work given enough time (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

**Scenario 4 — The Machine God.** AI reaches AGI and some form of sentience, then helps design still-smarter AI — superintelligence emerges, and human supremacy over decision-making ends. Mollick is explicit that this need not mean the end of humanity, and could be a better world if the AI is properly aligned to human interests — but nobody actually knows, and the range of outcomes spans "machines of loving grace" caretaking humanity to viewing humans as a threat, inconvenience, or resource. He cites Geoffrey Hinton's 2023 departure from the field as a credibility marker for taking the risk seriously, and references p(doom) (researchers' estimated probability AI leads to human extinction) as the framing serious AI safety researchers use (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

## Eucatastrophe: the deliberate aim

Mollick's own stance, stated directly: dwelling on Scenario 4 risks inducing learned helplessness — if you only consider one giant AI apocalypse, you lose the ability to act on the more likely Scenarios 2 and 3, where humans still have agency over outcomes. He redirects attention to the "many small catastrophes" AI can cause well short of an apocalypse — unimaginative leaders using AI for surveillance or layoffs, AI-driven harm concentrated on the less fortunate in developing countries, educators using AI in ways that leave some students behind.

He borrows Tolkien's term **eucatastrophe** — "the good catastrophe," a sudden joyous turn that is never guaranteed but can be aimed for — to argue these outcomes are not predetermined. Decisions about widely-applicable technology aren't made by a small group; people throughout organizations shape what AI means for their team, customers, and students. The chapter's closing line: "We need to aim for eucatastrophe, lest our inaction makes catastrophe inevitable" (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

## Epilogue: AI as Us

The book's short closing meditation: AI is alien yet deeply human — trained on humanity's cultural history, aligned to human goals via reinforcement learning from human feedback, carrying human biases, and built through a real mix of idealism, entrepreneurial ambition, and exploitation of labor. Mollick frames AI as a mirror reflecting back humanity's best and worst qualities, with the choices made about its use — not the technology itself — determining what it ultimately does for or to humanity.

As a structural close, Mollick has the AI complete the book's final paragraph itself (prompted: "Complete this, beautifully, fittingly, and well"), producing a deliberately over-wrought passage about being "a glimmer, an echo of humankind... a canvas, awaiting the brushstrokes of human hands." He immediately undercuts the tone — "Okay. That was pretty corny." — using the bathos itself to make the book's thesis concrete: AI is a co-intelligence, not an autonomous mind, and humans remain far from obsolete, at least for now (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).
