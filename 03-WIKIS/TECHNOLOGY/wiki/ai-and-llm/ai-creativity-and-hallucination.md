# AI Creativity and Hallucination

**Summary**: Hallucination and creativity are two faces of the same mechanism — an LLM generating plausible-sounding novel text with no concept of truth. This is what makes AI unreliable for precision work and unexpectedly strong at idea generation, and it's reshaping what "creative work" even means once a single button can produce a passable first draft.

**Sources**: CoIntelligence.pdf (Chapter 5, "AI as a Creative")

**Last updated**: 2026-06-17

---

## Hallucination is structural, not a bug

LLMs predict the statistically most likely next token; they have no internal concept of true vs. false, and no way to retrieve information from a "memory" — facts are reconstructed from learned patterns, not looked up. This means hallucination isn't a glitch to be patched out; it's intrinsic to how the model works (source: CoIntelligence.pdf, building on [[llm-fundamentals]]).

Two illustrative cases:
- Asked for a "random" number between 1 and 100, ChatGPT answers "42" about 10% of the time (vs. the 1% a true random process would produce) — a residue of *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* references saturating its training data, while the model hallucinates that it's actually choosing randomly.
- In 2023, lawyer Steven A. Schwartz used ChatGPT to research a legal brief; the model fabricated six (in fact, more) entirely fake case citations, which Schwartz submitted to a real court without verification. The judge fined him for bad faith. Mollick notes that even his own fact-checked retelling of this story, generated by GPT-4 with internet access, contained additional small errors — "it isn't the big issues you catch but the small ones you don't notice that cause problems" (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).

Hallucination rates are dropping with scale (GPT-3.5 erred in 98% of citations tested in one study, GPT-4 in 20%), but Principle 4 — assume this is the worst AI you'll use — cuts both ways: today's rate is a floor to improve from, not a number to design policy around yet. See [[four-rules-for-co-intelligence]] Principle 2 for the human-in-the-loop response to this risk.

## The same mechanism makes AI good at idea generation

Hallucination — finding novel connections outside a strict reading of training data — is also what lets an LLM perform tasks it was never explicitly trained for (e.g., generating a vowel-only sentence about an elephant on the moon). Mollick frames this as the "paradox of AI creativity": the feature that makes LLMs unreliable for factual precision is the same one that makes them useful for generating ideas.

On standard psychological creativity tests, AI performs strongly:
- **Alternative Uses Test (AUT)** — generate varied uses for a common object. Mollick generated 122 ideas for a toothbrush in two minutes; a typical human generates 5–10. A Haase/Hanel study blind-judging AUT output found GPT-4 outperformed all but 9.4% of 100 human testers.
- **Remote Associates Test (RAT)** — find the word connecting three seemingly unrelated words. AI "usually maxes out" this test, consistent with its nature as a connection machine.
- **Practical idea generation**: in a Wharton innovation-class contest (GPT-4 vs. 200 students producing ideas for a $50-or-less student product), AI ideas attracted more buyer interest from human judges; 35 of the top 40 rated ideas came from ChatGPT.

The caveat: AI raises the floor more than the ceiling. The most innovative humans benefit least from AI help, because **very creative people generate both more and better ideas than others** (the "equal-odds rule") — and AI's default output, without deliberate prompting for variance, tends toward same-y, "average" ideas drawn from its training distribution. The fix is the same persona/constraint technique from [[four-rules-for-co-intelligence]] Principle 3: explicitly ask for a high-variance, weird, or expert-framed response rather than accepting the default.

## "The Button" and the crisis of meaning in creative work

A large amount of ordinary work — marketing copy, performance reviews, strategic memos, letters of recommendation — has historically signaled quality *through the time invested in it*. Once every office tool ships a one-click AI draft button, that signal breaks: AI-generated output can be both faster and, by most human judgment, better than what an unmotivated or unskilled writer would produce unaided (an MIT study by Noy & Zhang found ChatGPT cut task time by 37% while *increasing* judged quality, with the biggest gains for the weakest performers — narrowing inequality between high and low scorers).

Mollick names two costs of reflexively pushing The Button: (1) **anchoring** — even heavily edited AI-influenced drafts stay tainted by the AI's first framing, narrowing the range of alternatives actually considered; (2) **shallower thinking** — when AI does the synthesis, the human skips the critical/reflective work that drafting was always partly *for*. The MIT study found most participants didn't even bother editing AI output once generated — a direct empirical instance of failing [[four-rules-for-co-intelligence]] Principle 2.

The deeper, harder-to-fix problem: tasks like letters of recommendation were valuable specifically *because* they were time-consuming — the time spent was the signal of genuine support. AI breaks that signal everywhere it appears (creating documents that get sent to AI-powered inboxes and assessed by other AIs, a hollowing-out Mollick calls "mere ceremony"). His prediction, by analogy to musicians shifting to live performance after recorded music and photographers pushing past realism after the camera: society will need to reconstruct what signals genuine effort and meaning in creative work, not simply lose it (source: CoIntelligence.pdf).
