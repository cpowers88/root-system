# The Four Types of Work (IT Operations)

**Summary**: The Phoenix Project's diagnostic framework for why IT Operations is chronically overloaded: all demand on an IT org falls into one of four categories, and most organizations can only see/manage one or two of them, leaving the rest invisible until they cause a crisis.

**Sources**: The Phoenix Project, thePhoenixProject.pdf (named on p. 92; fourth category confirmed pp. 157-159; Resource Guide "The Four Types of Work", pp. 362-366)

**Last updated**: 2026-06-18

---

## The four categories

Erik Reid names the framework directly but only gives Bill one category for free, forcing him to identify the rest: *"there are four types of work. You've named business project work as one. When you have the other three, give me a call"* (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, p. 92).

The Resource Guide later formalizes the categories Bill has been forced to discover:

1. **Business projects** - business initiatives, usually tracked by the Project Management Office as the organization's official project portfolio. At Parts Unlimited, this is the visible work Kirsten tracks and executives fight over (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, p. 362).
2. **Internal IT projects** - infrastructure, operations, or improvement work generated by IT itself, including work business projects require indirectly, such as environment creation or deployment automation. This is often scattered across budget owners rather than centrally tracked, which becomes dangerous when IT Operations is the bottleneck (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, p. 362).
3. **Changes** - work generated by business projects and internal IT projects, often tracked in different systems for different parts of the value stream, such as Remedy for IT Operations and JIRA or an Agile planning tool for Development. Split tracking creates handoff blindness; a dedicated product/service team can reduce that blindness by keeping operational incidents, defects, and features in the same backlog (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, p. 363).
4. **Unplanned work / recovery work** - operational incidents and problems, often caused by the other three types of work, that consume capacity at the expense of planned commitments (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, p. 363).

## The fourth category, confirmed: unplanned work as "antimatter"

Bill identifies the fourth category himself a week after Erik names the puzzle, triggered by a very concrete clue: the change-coordination whiteboard, which had reliably shown 40-50 completed changes per day, suddenly went completely blank for several days. Every change card had been pulled to a "Changes To Be Rescheduled" pile because the entire organization was firefighting the Phoenix launch disaster instead (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, pp. 156-158).

The mechanism is not that unplanned work is one priority among several. It is categorically destructive to the other three categories, because anything still in progress gets dropped, not merely delayed, the moment a fire starts (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, p. 159). This reframes most of what Bill had already been doing by instinct - coordinating changes carefully, protecting Brent from random escalations, containing outages quickly - as all serving the same underlying goal: minimizing unplanned work's ability to detonate everything else.

## Why this framework matters

The core failure mode the book dramatizes: organizations build visibility into one category, usually business projects via a PMO, and have none into the other three. This means:

- Resourcing decisions get made without seeing the full demand picture.
- The same people get assigned to projects, internal IT work, and incident response with no mechanism to see they are triple-booked until delivery fails.
- Unplanned work is invisible until it happens, so it can never be planned around; it silently consumes whatever capacity was allocated to planned commitments.

The Resource Guide adds the operating consequence: when a constrained work center is heavily utilized, work spends most of its lead time waiting in queue rather than being actively worked. A 30-minute task that crosses seven 90%-busy work centers can accumulate roughly 63 hours of queue time, meaning touch time is a tiny fraction of total lead time (source: thePhoenixProject.pdf, pp. 364-366). This is why making all four work types visible and limiting WIP is not administrative neatness; it is the only way to keep lead time from exploding.
