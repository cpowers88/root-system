# Data as a Strategic Asset: How Signet Bank Bought the Data It Needed to Become Capital One

**Summary**: Data and the capability to extract knowledge from it are best understood as a *deliberate investment*, not a byproduct you passively accumulate — illustrated by little Signet Bank's decision to deliberately lose money offering randomized credit terms purely to generate the profitability data no one in the industry had, a bet that turned into Capital One. Closes Chapter 1 with the managerial case for why understanding data science matters even if you'll never build a model yourself.

**Sources**: DataScienceforBusiness.pdf (Provost & Fawcett, *Data Science for Business*, O'Reilly, 2013), Chapter 1 ("Introduction: Data-Analytic Thinking")

**Last updated**: 2026-06-22

## The Core Reframe: Data Is an Asset You Invest In, Not Something You Just Have

**The chapter's central strategic principle, worth keeping as the standing lens for any client data conversation**: data, and the organizational capability to extract useful knowledge from it, should be regarded as **key strategic assets** — which immediately raises the question every other asset class forces you to ask: how much should we invest in acquiring or improving this asset, and is that investment worth it? **The complementary-assets point is easy to miss but important**: "the best data science team can yield little value without the appropriate data; the right data often cannot substantially improve decisions without suitable data science talent" — meaning a client who has hired strong analytical talent but starves them of the right data is making the identical mistake as a client who has rich data but no one capable of extracting value from it. **Both halves have to be funded together, or neither pays off.**

## The Signet Bank Case: Deliberately Losing Money to Buy the Data That Didn't Exist

**The strategic problem, stated precisely**: in the early 1990s, virtually all credit cards used uniform pricing — not because differentiated pricing wasn't a good idea, but because (1) no bank's information systems could support differential pricing at scale, and (2) management broadly believed customers wouldn't tolerate price discrimination anyway. Two visionaries (Richard Fairbanks and Nigel Morris) believed sophisticated predictive modeling could support genuinely differentiated terms — but every major bank they pitched turned them down, since none could see how to actually execute the idea. **Small, regional Signet Bank finally took the bet** — convinced, correctly, that modeling *profitability* (not just default risk) was the better strategy, since a small fraction of customers generate more than 100% of a credit card business's profit (the rest break even or lose money).

**The specific data problem this revealed, worth keeping as a generalizable diagnostic**: **no bank anywhere had the data needed to model profitability under terms different from the ones actually offered** — every bank's historical data only covered (1) the terms it had actually offered in the past, and (2) the customers its existing default model had already deemed creditworthy enough to approve. There was no way to model "what would profitability look like under a different set of terms, offered to a different population" using only data generated by the current policy — **a closed information loop that perfectly preserved the status quo.**

**Signet's actual solution**: deliberately acquire the missing data **at a real, measured cost** — by randomly offering different credit terms to different customers and watching what happened, fully aware this would generate short-term losses. **The cost was real and significant**: Signet's charge-off rate (the fraction of balances that go unpaid) jumped from an industry-leading 2.9% to almost 6% as soon as randomized terms started going out, and losses continued for several years while the resulting data was used to build, evaluate, and deploy improved models. **The decisive organizational discipline, worth keeping as the sharpest single lesson from this case**: management treated these losses explicitly as **an investment in a data asset**, not an operational failure — and that explicit framing is exactly what let them persist through years of stakeholder complaints rather than abandoning the experiment at the first sign of worse numbers. **A client team that doesn't have this explicit asset-investment framing in place is far more likely to kill a similar experiment the moment short-term metrics worsen, even when the experiment is working exactly as designed.**

**The outcome**: Signet's credit card operation eventually became so profitable it was spun off — becoming **Capital One**, with Fairbanks and Morris as Chairman/CEO and President/COO, applying the same data-investment philosophy to customer retention as well as acquisition (live, model-driven offer optimization at the point of a retention call). By 2000, Capital One was reportedly running **45,000 "scientific tests"** simultaneously — the randomized-experimentation-as-deliberate-data-acquisition strategy, scaled from one initial bet into the company's permanent operating model.

## Confirming Evidence: Bigger, Richer Data Assets Really Do Compound

**Martens and Provost (2011)** ran a directly relevant follow-up study at a real bank, examining what kinds of customer data actually improve predictive models for product targeting. **Sociodemographic data** (the traditional, easy-to-obtain category) provided real predictive value — but **hit a ceiling**: beyond a certain volume, additional sociodemographic data conferred no further advantage. **Detailed individual transaction data**, by contrast, improved predictive performance substantially beyond sociodemographic data alone, and — critically — **kept improving as more transaction data was added, with no sign of leveling off anywhere in the range the study examined.** **The direct strategic implication, worth keeping as a standing argument for any client weighing whether richer data collection is worth the investment**: a larger, deeper *transactional* data asset can produce a durable, compounding strategic advantage over smaller competitors in a way that simply accumulating more of an already-saturated, easy-to-obtain data category (demographics) cannot.

**Other named examples reinforcing the same principle, worth keeping as quick, recognizable client-facing illustrations**: Amazon's early customer ratings/reviews data created genuine switching costs (customers value Amazon's resulting recommendations enough to tolerate a price premium); Harrah's casino-loyalty data investment took it from a minor casino operator to (via the 2005 Caesars acquisition) the world's largest gambling company; Facebook's valuation has been explicitly tied to its unique social-graph data asset, with academic evidence (Hill, Provost, and Volinsky 2006) that network-structure information specifically improves predictive models of who will buy a given product, beyond what individual-level data alone can do.

## Why This Matters Even If You'll Never Build a Model Yourself

**The chapter's explicit, repeated point, worth keeping as the justification for why a non-technical business owner or manager should bother learning any of this**: understanding data science is valuable **even if you never personally intend to do it** — because it equips you to evaluate proposals (from an employee, a consultant, or an acquisition target) systematically, spotting unrealistic assumptions or missing pieces, even without being able to predict whether a given project will actually succeed (which, for real data mining work, "often requires trying"). **The McKinsey workforce-gap statistic cited directly** is a striking, quotable number: a projected US shortage (by 2018) of 140,000-190,000 people with deep analytical skills, **alongside a shortage of 1.5 million managers and analysts who simply understand enough to use analytical output effectively.** The ratio (roughly 10 managers needed per deep specialist) isn't because data scientists are unusually hard to manage — it's because **a single data science team's leverage only gets realized across the many business units that have to understand and act on its output**, and that understanding is the actual bottleneck, not the specialist talent itself.

**The chemistry analogy, worth keeping as a precise way to frame data science's current maturity to a skeptical client**: data science today resembles 19th-century chemistry — genuine, durable underlying principles are just beginning to crystallize out of what is still a largely experimental, tool-driven field, and (just as every good 19th-century chemist had to also be a competent lab technician) a working data scientist today has to be reasonably fluent with specific software tools. **But the book is explicit that its own focus, and the durable value for a business reader, is the *science* — the principles — not the *tools*, which will keep changing on a much shorter cycle than the underlying concepts.**

## North Star Connection

- How this applies to the audit business: the "data is an asset you deliberately invest in, with a measurable cost" framing is a directly transferable argument for recommending a client run a genuine, controlled experiment (even a costly one) to fill a real information gap, rather than relying on whatever data the current process happens to already produce. The Martens & Provost transaction-data finding supports a specific, evidence-based recommendation: when advising a client on what new data to start collecting, transactional/behavioral detail is a better long-term investment than simply acquiring more of an already-saturated demographic category.

## Use / Retrieval Notes

**Best use**:
Business-case and client-communication tool — use the Signet Bank/Capital One story to illustrate the principle of deliberately investing in (and absorbing the short-term cost of) acquiring missing data, and cite the Martens & Provost finding when recommending what type of new data a client should prioritize collecting.

**Use when**:
Pitching a client on running a real experiment or investing in new data collection to fill an identified information gap, or explaining why "we don't have the data to know that yet" is itself a fixable, investable problem rather than a permanent limitation.

**Do not use when**:
The client's actual problem is a talent/capability gap rather than a data gap — per the complementary-assets point, recommending more data investment when the real bottleneck is analytical talent (or vice versa) wastes the recommendation.
