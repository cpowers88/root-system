# Board Oversight Crisis, Information, and Coalition Power

**Summary**: A board can hold formal authority to remove a chief executive and
still lack the information system, succession coalition, and stakeholder
legitimacy needed to make that authority durable. OpenAI's 2023 board crisis
combined a real oversight problem with a failed execution: directors accumulated
serious concerns through informal channels, acted in secrecy to prevent
interference, and then could not explain their decision without exposing sources.
Employees, executives, investors, and Microsoft rapidly formed a stronger
operating coalition and reversed the outcome.

**Source**: `raw/empireofAIDreamsandNightmares.pdf` (Karen Hao, *Empire of AI:
Dreams and Nightmares in Sam Altman's OpenAI*, 2025), Chapter 15, “The Gambit”
(physical PDF pp. 326-342), and Chapter 16, “Cloak-and-Dagger” (pp. 343-357),
reviewed as two complete connected chunks. Chapter 17 begins on physical p. 358;
boundary visually verified.

**Last updated**: 2026-07-16

## Source Posture

This page synthesizes Hao's investigative reconstruction from interviews,
documents, and reported private discussions. OpenAI and Altman did not cooperate
with the book. Treat dialogue, motives, and descriptions of individual behavior
as attributed reporting. The durable retrieval target is the governance mechanism,
not a verdict on any person's character.

## Management Information Was Fragmented by Design

Hao portrays a leadership system in which the CEO often communicated verbally,
gave different messages to different parties, and remained the only person with a
complete view of commitments. Executives could not reliably determine whether a
change reflected a reasoned decision, partner pressure, or a private promise.

This information topology creates control through fragmentation:

`team A -> CEO <- team B`

`board -> CEO <- partner`

Each group believes it has alignment with the center but cannot compare its
understanding with the others. A coordinating executive can spend enormous time
repairing contradictions without resolving the source. The organization becomes
dependent on the same person whose decisions it is trying to verify.

## Informal Escalation Is Not an Oversight System

Senior leaders approached individual directors because routine channels did not
feel safe or effective. The board pieced together concerns from private calls,
personal relationships, disappearing messages, and dossiers. Directors reportedly
heard similar concerns from multiple senior leaders and also had their own
questions about conflicts, partner protocol breaches, board appointments, and the
Startup Fund's ownership.

Cross-source corroboration is valuable, but the process had structural weaknesses:

- no standing adverse-information channel;
- no protected, documented escalation procedure;
- inconsistent direct access to executives and employees;
- dependence on personal trust between a source and one director;
- difficulty distinguishing conduct, performance, safety, and governance claims;
- no agreed process for investigation before a binary CEO decision.

The board excluded disputed family allegations from its professional assessment,
which is the correct boundary for an institutional decision unless independently
relevant evidence is established.

## Secrecy Protected the Decision and Weakened It

Directors feared that a broader fact-finding process or early consultation with
Microsoft and senior staff would allow management to block their deliberation.
They therefore limited the circle, removed Altman, and installed an interim CEO
before a wider coalition understood the case.

This reduced interference risk but created a legitimacy deficit. The public phrase
“not consistently candid” was too vague to justify a decision of that magnitude.
Directors believed that offering more detail would expose confidential sources.
Executives and employees interpreted the information gap as evidence that the
board lacked a sound basis.

A board facing confidential evidence needs a disclosure ladder before acting:

1. full confidential record for directors and independent counsel;
2. source-protecting findings for affected executives;
3. substantiated categories and examples for employees and critical partners;
4. a public explanation proportionate to the action;
5. a later independent report with necessary redactions.

## Formal Authority Lost to Coalition Power

The board could legally fire the CEO. It could not ensure that the company would
continue functioning afterward. The counter-coalition held stronger practical
assets:

- executives with operating legitimacy;
- employees whose labor and equity could leave together;
- Microsoft as cloud, capital, and potential employer;
- investors focused on company value and an active tender offer;
- founder and investor networks able to coordinate rapidly;
- alternative labs ready to recruit displaced talent.

The interim CEO and Sutskever changed position when preservation of OpenAI
appeared incompatible with the board's decision. Their reversal was not merely
personal inconsistency; it exposed the absence of a prepared operating coalition
for the alternative leadership plan.

## Succession Is a System, Not a Name

The board reasoned that Murati could run daily operations and maintain the
Microsoft relationship. It did not secure in advance:

- her durable commitment under stakeholder pressure;
- a communications and evidence plan she could defend;
- backing from the rest of the executive team;
- Microsoft and investor continuity;
- employee retention terms and mission legitimacy;
- a second succession option if she withdrew.

An interim appointment without those elements is a candidate, not a succession
plan.

## Investigation Without Publication Restores Little Trust

The post-crisis review reportedly examined thousands of documents and dozens of
interviews, but its report was not released to employees or the public. The new
board announced confidence in Altman and Brockman while former directors
reiterated their concerns. With evidence and reasoning kept confidential, each
side's supporters could preserve their original interpretation.

Independent review must define its question carefully. A review limited to whether
the board followed a good process is different from one examining management
conduct, disclosure failures, conflicts, partner breaches, or mission compliance.
Scope, standard of proof, findings, remediation, and publication policy should be
specified before the investigation begins.

## Board-Crisis Readiness Gate

Before a mission board needs to act, require:

1. independent counsel and budget controlled by the board;
2. routine written reporting, conflicts disclosure, and partner-protocol reporting;
3. protected employee/executive escalation to multiple independent directors;
4. evidence preservation and an investigation ladder short of immediate removal;
5. source-protecting disclosure templates for executives, staff, partners, and the
   public;
6. at least two succession candidates with tested stakeholder support;
7. a practical power map covering employees, equity, investors, infrastructure,
   customers, and partner alternatives;
8. pre-agreed investigation scope and publication rules after an emergency action.
