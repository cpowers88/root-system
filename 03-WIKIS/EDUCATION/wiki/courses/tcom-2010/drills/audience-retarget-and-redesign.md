# Drill — Audience Retargeting and Document Redesign

## Use point

**Do not complete this now.** Part A fits Week 2 (audience analysis). Part B fits
Weeks 4–5 (proposal). Part C fits Week 6 (document design) and again before the
report draft in Weeks 6–12.

Each part is **generative retrieval matched to the required performance** — you
produce a document for a named reader rather than recalling a definition. That is
the retrieval type that matches how TCOM is actually assessed.

## Part A — Same facts, three readers

Take any short technical fact you already know well (how a `for` loop works, what
net force means, how a circuit breaker trips — the subject does not matter; the
retargeting does).

Write **three** short paragraphs conveying it to:

1. A **decision-maker** who controls a budget and has no technical background.
2. A **technician** who must act on it today.
3. A **classmate** in the same course.

Then answer, without notes:

- Which paragraph changed the *most* from the others, and was it vocabulary,
  ordering, or amount of background?
- Which reader needed the conclusion first, and why?
- What did you leave out for reader 1 that reader 2 required?

## Part B — Reverse-engineer a proposal

Open one worked example — `raw\Linked-Resources\Examples\Proposal_Dog-Park.doc` or
`Proposal_Movies.doc`. Read it once, then **close it.**

From memory:

1. List the sections in the order they appeared.
2. For each, write one line on what job it does for the reader.
3. Name the problem the proposal claims to solve, and the evidence offered.
4. Name one assumption that, if false, would sink the proposal.

Reopen and compare. **The gap between your list and the real one is the finding** —
inspect it rather than just correcting it.

## Part C — Redesign for the eye

Find any badly formatted page — a dense syllabus, a wall-of-text web page, one of
`raw\Linked-Resources\4-7_Bad-Graphs.pdf`.

1. Before changing anything, predict which single change will most improve it.
2. Redesign it using headings, white space, and lists only — no new content.
3. Test the prediction: was the biggest gain the one you named?
4. State what the *original* design implied about who its reader was.

For the graphs specifically: name the technique that misleads, and say whether it
misleads through selection, emphasis, omission, or visual distortion. That
vocabulary is the Week 1 ethics material, reused where it actually bites.

## Higher-order close

After any part, answer one:

- What would make this document fail with a reader you have not considered?
- If the deadline halved, which section would you cut, and what would break?
- Which choice here would you defend to a client paying for it?
