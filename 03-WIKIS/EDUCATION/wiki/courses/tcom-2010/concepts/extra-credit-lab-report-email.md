# Extra Credit — Lab Report Email

### Built 2026-08-30 from the D2L rubric (`rubric_extra_credit_lab_email.pdf`, captured 2026-08-29). **Due Tue Dec 1, 2026. Extra credit is never accepted late, no exceptions.** 11 points on the rubric; extra credit can move the **final course grade by up to 2 points**.

> **What this assignment actually is, in one sentence:**
> an **email to Professor Diamond**, written in the shape of a **lab report**,
> reporting on the **usability test that was run on Chris's own instruction set**
> on **Lab Day, Thu Nov 19**.

The word "lab" is doing all the confusing work. There is no laboratory. The "lab"
is the usability test in Week 13 — Chris hands his polished instructions to a
classmate, watches them try to perform the task, and records what happens. This
email is the write-up of that observation, using the sections a scientific lab
report uses: Materials, Methods, Results & Discussion, Conclusion.

---

## The prerequisite nobody states out loud

**This assignment cannot be written unless Lab Day actually happened to him.**

| Date | What must exist first |
|---|---|
| **Tue Nov 17** | Instructions Steps assignment written in class |
| **Thu Nov 19** | **LAB DAY.** Bring a polished version of the instructions, **all equipment** needed to perform them in class, and a **printed** Usability Worksheet |
| Nov 24–26 | Fall Break — no class |
| **Tue Dec 1** | **This email is due** |

The Usability Worksheet he fills out on Nov 19 **is the raw data for this email.**
Miss Lab Day, or fill the worksheet in thinly, and there is nothing to report from
on Dec 1. Two rubric rows (3.25 points, ~30%) explicitly award **zero** for writing
about someone else's lab.

**Action on Nov 19:** the worksheet is not a formality to be handed in and
forgotten — it is the notes file for a graded document twelve days later. Photograph
or scan it the same day.

## The nine rubric rows

Total **11 points**. Bands: **Level 2 = 8 points minimum · Level 1 = 5 minimum · Level 0 = 0.**

| # | Row | Pts | Level 2 requires |
|---:|---|---:|---|
| 1 | Opening **AND** closing greeting | 0.5 | Opening at the **top**, closing at the **bottom**. Opening is `Dear Professor Diamond,` |
| 2 | Opening paragraph | 1.0 | **Why are you writing me** AND **what were you attempting to learn** — both halves |
| 3 | Signature block | 0.25 | Student name and email address **on different lines** |
| 4 | Materials & Methods | 1.5 | **One short descriptive paragraph each** — Materials, then Methods |
| 5 | Results & Discussion | 2.25 | Short descriptive paragraphs **describing and discussing in detail** the results |
| 6 | Conclusion | 1.0 | Present, and about **his own** lab |
| 7 | Grammar, punctuation, formatting, phrasing | 2.5 | *"work is a pleasure to read"* |
| 8 | Spacing | 1.0 | **1 space after a period** · **single-spaced** · **extra line space between paragraphs** |
| 9 | Reflection / AI Statement | 1.0 | See the AI section below — this row has a trap |

### The shape of the point allocation is the real lesson

**Rows 7 and 8 — grammar, punctuation, formatting, and spacing — are 3.5 of 11 points (32%).**
Results & Discussion, the entire substance of the report, is 2.25 (20%).

*Mechanics outweigh content by more than 50% on this rubric.* This is the
"follow directions" course stating its thesis in point values. A perfectly
observed usability test written with two spaces after periods and no blank
lines between paragraphs scores worse than a thin report typed correctly.

## What goes in each section

### Opening paragraph (1.0) — two questions, both answered

The rubric asks for two things and Level 1 is explicitly *"missing some information."*

1. **Why are you writing me** — to report the results of the usability test
   conducted on my instruction set during the Nov 19 lab.
2. **What were you attempting to learn** — the testing goal. Not "to do the
   assignment." Something like: whether a non-specialist reader could complete
   the task using only the written steps, and where the instructions broke down.

### Materials (part of 1.5) — one short paragraph

What was physically in the room and in the test.

- The instruction set itself (title, task, length, what visuals it carried)
- The equipment the task required — the "all equipment necessary" the syllabus makes him bring
- The printed Usability Worksheet
- The test subject, described by **profile, not name-and-friendship**: a classmate
  who had not seen the instructions before and had no experience with the task

### Methods (part of 1.5) — one short paragraph

**What he did, in order.** This is procedure, not results.

The course's own usability protocol supplies the method, and using its actual
moves is what makes this paragraph read as technical rather than casual:

- Instructions handed over **face down**; the user is not allowed to pre-read
- Pre-test questions asked and answers recorded (first thing seen, who the
  audience appears to be, whether it looks easy to follow)
- User instructed to **follow the steps exactly as written, even where they make
  no sense**, and to **talk continuously** while working
- Observer records everything said and done, and **does not answer questions**
  unless the user is genuinely stuck
- Post-test questions asked and recorded

### Results & Discussion (2.25) — the biggest row, and it wants two things

The rubric says **"describing & discussing in detail."** Those are separate jobs
and a report that only describes will land in the 0.01–1.12 band.

- **Describe:** what actually happened. Where the user hesitated, what they
  skipped, what they misread, what they got wrong, how long a step took, whether
  they finished. Quote them where the quote is the evidence.
- **Discuss:** what it *means about the instructions.* A user who reversed two
  steps points at ordering; a user who asked "which one is that?" points at a
  missing visual or an unlabeled part; a user who did something dangerous points
  at a missing caution. **The failure is in the document, not the reader** — that
  framing is the whole point of usability testing and is what she is grading.

Use short paragraphs, plural. One dense block is harder to read and row 7 is
watching.

### Conclusion (1.0)

What he will change in the instruction set as a result, and what the test showed
about the document overall. It needs to conclude about **his** lab — the row
awards zero for "concluding someone else's lab."

Note the useful overlap: the **Instructions Group Project is due Thu Dec 3**, two
days after this email. The revisions named in this conclusion are revisions he is
about to make anyway. Written once, used twice.

### Signature block (0.25)

```
Sincerely,

Chris Powers
<KSU email address>
```

**Name and email on separate lines.** Same line = 0.001–0.125. It is a quarter
point and it is free.

## ⚠ The AI Statement row is not permission to use AI

Row 9 offers two paths:

- **Did not use AI** → a brief paragraph of reflection on the assignment.
- **Did use AI** → state *what AI you used, what your prompt was, what you adapted
  from the output, and 2 reliable sources you used to verify the output.*

**Read that against the §04 syllabus, which is unchanged and blanket:**

> *"Using AI to write your assignments is considered PLAGIARISM. You may only use
> AI for editing or proofreading, if you cite the usage."*

**Disclosure is not permission.** The rubric row is the *citation mechanism* for
the editing and proofreading the syllabus already allows — it is not a second
policy that legalizes drafting. An AI-drafted email disclosed in full is still
plagiarism by her own written definition, and she states that misunderstanding
what counts is not accepted as a defence.

**Operationally, for this assignment:**

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| Chris drafts every paragraph; AI proofreads and he cites it in row 9 | AI drafts any paragraph, disclosed or not |
| AI explains what "Materials & Methods" means (this page) | AI writes the Materials & Methods paragraphs |
| AI quizzes him on the rubric before he submits | AI produces an outline that becomes the submission |

If he writes it himself — the correct path — row 9 is the **reflection paragraph**,
and it is the cheaper of the two options: one short paragraph versus four required
disclosures plus two verification sources.

## Spacing (1.0) — the row that is pure mechanical compliance

Three named requirements, all checkable before sending:

1. **One space after a period.** Not two. Word's *Find & Replace* on `". "` → `". "` catches it.
2. **Single-spaced** document.
3. **An extra line space between paragraphs** — a blank line, not an indent.

In email this means: no first-line indents, a blank line between every paragraph,
and no double-returns inside a paragraph.

## ⚠ Open item — verify the submission channel before Dec 1

**Do not assume email.** Two sources point in different directions:

| Source | Says |
|---|---|
| Syllabus schedule + [[courses/tcom-2010/concepts/course-policies-and-file-naming]] | **Email** — the deliverable is the email itself |
| The rubric PDF's own URL | printed from a **D2L dropbox submit-files page** (`dropbox/user/folder_submit_files.d2l?db=4336326`) — meaning a D2L folder for this exists |

Both can be true — she may want the email body pasted or attached into a dropbox —
but "lab report **email**" submitted to a **dropbox** is exactly the shape of the
Business Email contradiction that cost two failed cold reps in August. The standing
rule settles it: **read the live assignment's SUBMISSION section in D2L**, and let
the assignment file outrank the syllabus. Never infer the channel from the genre.

If it is a D2L file submission and no filename string is printed, the documented
fallback is `Powers_04_AssignmentName.docx` — used only after the SUBMISSION
section genuinely fails to specify.

## Why this transfers

Usability testing is the one method in TCOM that is directly billable: it is how
you find out whether a client's documentation, onboarding, or process actually
works for the person who has to use it, before it ships. The report structure here
— what we tested, how, what broke, what we recommend — is the same structure as a
client findings memo.
