## Chapter 2 — Fundamental Economic Questions and Gains from Specialization

### The actors and the inputs

**goods and services** — outputs of the production process: food, clothing, shelter,
healthcare, education, entertainment.

**factors of production** — inputs to the production process, broadly categorized as
**land, labor, and capital**.

**production** — the process by which inputs (factors of production) are transformed into
an output (a good or service).

**households** — the decision-making entities whose primary objective is to obtain benefits
from *consuming* goods and services.

**firms** — the decision-making entities whose primary role is to *produce* goods and
services for consumption by households.

> **Anchor:** the Circular Flow Diagram is a jobsite payroll loop — households sell labor to
> firms, firms sell output back to households, and the same dollars go around twice.

### The three fundamental economic questions

Every society must address these when deciding how to use scarce productive resources:

| # | Question | Name of the decision |
|---|---|---|
| i | What to produce? | **production decision** |
| ii | How to produce it? (which resources) | **resource use decision** |
| iii | For whom to produce it? | **distributional decision** |

> **This table is the single highest-yield item in Chapter 2.** Her questions test the
> *pairing* — she gives a scenario and asks which of the three it is. Learn the name attached
> to each question, not just the list of three.

**Common confusion:** "How can we reduce our carbon footprint?" and "How can we guarantee
healthcare access?" are **not** among the three. Only the what/how/for-whom triple counts.

### The Production Possibilities Frontier

**production possibilities frontier (PPF)** — a curve that summarizes the limits of
production a society faces, by illustrating the maximum amount of one good that can be
produced for every possible level of production of another good.

**attainable output combination** — a combination that *can possibly* be produced with
available resources and technology — **on or below** the PPF.

**unattainable output combination** — a combination that *cannot possibly* be produced with
available resources and technology — **beyond** the PPF.

**productive efficiency** — a situation in which it is **NOT** possible to increase the
amount produced of any good without decreasing the amount of some other good — the society
is producing **on** its PPF.

**productive inefficiency** — a situation in which it **is** possible to increase the amount
of some good without decreasing any other good — the society is producing **below** its PPF.

> **Anchor:** the PPF is the crew's full-day output ceiling. On the line, every extra sheet
> of drywall costs you framing time. Below the line, someone is standing around — you can get
> more of both. Beyond the line, you would need another crew.

**Common confusion — she tests this directly:** *attainable* does **not** mean *efficient*.
Every point on **or below** the curve is attainable; only points **on** it are efficient.
A question offering "all attainable combinations are characterized by productive efficiency"
is false.

### Advantage and opportunity cost

**absolute advantage** — a producer has an absolute advantage in producing a good if she can
produce **more output** than another producer **using the same amount of inputs**.

**opportunity cost** — the cost of giving up the **best alternative** that must be foregone
in order to do or acquire something; it measures the value of the **next best** use of the
resources used.

**comparative advantage** — a producer has a comparative advantage in producing a good if
**her opportunity cost of producing that good is lower** than another producer's opportunity
cost of producing the same good.

**Law of Comparative Advantage** — a guide for allocating scarce productive resources: when
increasing the production of a good, a society should do so using the available productive
resource with the **lowest opportunity cost**. Applying this rule lets a society reach
productive efficiency.

> **Anchor:** you frame faster than the new guy *and* you trim faster than him — absolute
> advantage in both. But every hour you spend trimming costs the crew a wall that only you
> can frame. He has the lower opportunity cost on trim, so trim is his. **Absolute advantage
> in both is common; comparative advantage in both is impossible.**

**Common confusion — the one she builds distractors from:** knowing only that Mike assembles
20 bikes/day and Xavier 15 tells you Mike has the **absolute** advantage in bikes and
**nothing** about comparative advantage. Comparative advantage requires **both** goods'
numbers.

## The opportunity-cost computation — the skeleton

From her `Two person- two goods model.pdf`. This is the only arithmetic in Chapter 2, and it
is worth two to three quiz questions.

**Setup: a table of what each producer makes in one full workday.**

|  | Coconuts | Shrubberies |
|---|---|---|
| Arthur | 6 | 18 |
| Bedevere | 24 | 48 |

**Step 1 — absolute advantage:** read the columns. Bigger number wins that good.
*Bedevere wins both (24 > 6, 48 > 18).*

**Step 2 — opportunity cost, one producer at a time.** To get the OC of **one unit of A**,
divide **the other good by A**:

```
OC of one A  =  (units of B) / (units of A)
```

- Bedevere, one coconut → 48 / 24 = **2 shrubberies**
- Bedevere, one shrubbery → 24 / 48 = **½ coconut**
- Arthur, one coconut → 18 / 6 = **3 shrubberies**
- Arthur, one shrubbery → 6 / 18 = **⅓ coconut**

**Step 3 — comparative advantage:** compare the *same* good across producers; **lower OC
wins**.
- Coconuts: Bedevere 2 vs. Arthur 3 → **Bedevere**
- Shrubberies: Arthur ⅓ vs. Bedevere ½ → **Arthur**

> **The check that catches an arithmetic slip:** the two comparative advantages must land on
> **different people**. If one person "wins" both, the division was flipped.

> **Anchor for the flip:** the thing you *give up* goes on top. You are pricing a coconut
> *in shrubberies*, so shrubberies are the numerator.

## Chapter 3 — Economic Systems: Capitalism versus Socialism

**economic system** — the rules and methods put in place by a society to answer the three
fundamental economic questions.

**comparative economic systems** — the subfield of economics that compares and contrasts the
structure and performance of different types of economic organization.

### The four primary economic institutions

**households, firms, markets, and government.**

- **households** — the most fundamental part of any economic system; ultimate consumers of
  most finished goods/services; primary suppliers of labor.
- **firms** — the institutions which transform factors of production into finished
  goods/services.
- **market** — the collection of all potential buyers and all potential sellers of a good or
  service.
- **government** — a decision-making institution with the legal authority to impose
  restrictions or mandates on other decision-makers, i.e. **the ability to use legal
  coercion**.

**contract** — a legal document specifying what different parties must do whatever the
external circumstances, and providing enforcement or compensation for non-performance.

### The three kinds of economic resource

- **natural assets** — natural resources: minerals, naturally occurring vegetation, water,
  topographical features, agriculturally productive land.
- **produced assets** — currently available machines, factories, and inventories as
  industrial capital, plus social capital such as transportation and communications
  infrastructure and educational institutions.
- **human capital** — the skills, education, and training individuals in the labor force
  possess.

> **Her worked distractor set:** coal reserves = natural · a VW plant = produced ·
> a college education = human capital.

### The systems

- **capitalism** — means of production **privately owned** and operated for a profit.
- **socialism** — means of production **owned by the government**.
- **communism** — means of production **collectively owned by all people**, without
  intervention by a government or state. A stateless, classless system: *"From each according
  to his ability, to each according to his need."*
- **feudalism** — land ownership restricted to an aristocratic nobility.
- **mixed economy** — most factors of production owned and controlled by individuals, while
  **some** are owned and controlled by the state.

> **Common confusion she tests twice:** socialism is **state** ownership; communism is
> **collective ownership without a state**. The former Soviet Union is her example of a large
> society close to **pure socialism** — explicitly *not* Marx's communism.

### The three dimensions of private property

| Right | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **right to control** | the right to decide how to use your property |
| **right to transfer** | the right to obtain ownership from, or relinquish it to, another |
| **right to restitution** | the right to be compensated when another damages your property or infringes your rights |

> **Anchor (her own scenario shape):** a kid breaks your windshield and pays for it — that is
> **restitution**, not control or transfer.

**consumer sovereignty** — the freedom of an individual to choose to purchase, or not
purchase, a good or service at a price determined in a free, unfettered market.

### The two figures

- **Adam Smith** — **18th century Scottish** economist; wrote *An Inquiry into the Nature and
  Causes of the Wealth of Nations*. ⚠ **Her study guide prints the dates `(1721-1790)`; the
  standard reference dates are 1723–1790.** Recorded as she wrote it because her document is
  the exam standard — but the tested fact is *18th century / Scottish*, which is what her MCQ
  actually asks. Do not memorize the birth year either way.
- **Karl Marx** — **19th century (1818–1883) German** philosopher, economist, and
  revolutionary; wrote *Das Kapital* and co-wrote *The Communist Manifesto* with Engels.
  - **bourgeoisie** = business owners · **proletariat** = the working class.
- **Louis Blanc** — *The Organization of Work* (1839) is the actual source of *"From each
  according to his ability, to each according to his need."* **Not Marx** — she asks this.

**Invisible Hand** — Smith's recognition that under certain conditions, self-interested
decision makers interacting in free markets produce outcomes better for all parties. Where it
applies, any alternative to the market outcome would be less desirable for someone; free
market forces lead to an "efficient" outcome in that total social surplus is maximized.

### Human-nature assumptions

- **Economic Man (homo economicus)** — a person who is both **self-interested and rational**.
  The standard mainstream assumption.
- **New Soviet Man** — a person motivated primarily by **selfless benevolence**.

### Planning and incentives

- **command planning** — government directly controls nearly all economic activity; almost
  all production occurs in government-owned enterprises.
- **indicative planning** — government *guides* behavior by establishing policies that alter
  costs and benefits. *(Alec Nove: "the state uses influence, subsidies, grants, taxes, but
  does not compel.")*

**Three primary types of economic incentive:**

| Incentive | Definition |
|---|---|
| **material rewards** | monetary rewards or direct increases in consumption from an activity |
| **moral suasion** | convincing individuals to behave a certain way because it is the right thing to do |
| **coercion** | the use or threat of force or incarceration to obtain compliance |

> **Her worked distractor:** a governor offering a $100,000 reward is a **material
> incentive**, not coercion and not moral suasion.

### The three country indices — she cites textbook page numbers

| Index | Textbook location |
|---|---|
| Economic Freedom of the World | Table 3.1, **p. 55** |
| International Property Rights Index | Table 3.3, **p. 58** |

**These are lookups, not reasoning** — read the three tables in the eBook once before the
