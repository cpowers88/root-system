# Memory Techniques — matched to Chris's measured profile

### Distilled 2026-08-13 from four sources Chris routed through `77-INBOX`: Art of Memory's "36 Best Memory Techniques," Harvard Health's cueing article, Stanford CTL's working-memory strategies, and ADDA's "Top 10 Memory Strategies." Filtered through the YouScience aptitude model in `00-BRAIN\CHRIS.md` — techniques that fight his profile were dropped, not softened.

## Why these four sources agree with the vault

All four converge on what `learning-how-to-learn-principles.md` already holds: **active recall
beats rereading, associations beat repetition, sleep consolidates, and testing is learning.**
Nothing here contradicts the existing methods file. What these sources add is a **toolbox of
cue-construction techniques** — and cue construction is precisely Chris's lever, because his
one measured memory constraint is **cue-dependent associative memory** (Cue User): arbitrary
pairings — names, dates, vocabulary, formulas — don't stick from one exposure. Everything
else about his memory is normal to strong. **Build the cue, and the recall follows.**

## The match table — technique × aptitude × course

| Technique | What it is | Why it fits Chris | Where it pays this semester |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cueing / association** (Harvard) | At the moment of learning, deliberately attach the new item to something already known | **Direct hit on Cue User** — this is the named support in his own profile | Every new term in every course |
| **Memory palace / loci** (AoM, Stanford) | Store items at locations in a vividly imagined familiar place, walk the route to recall | **3D Visualizer** — mental rotation and spatial structure are top strengths; this technique runs *on* that hardware | Ordered lists: physics problem-solving steps, Python execution order, TCOM document sections |
| **Classroom-location variant** (ADDA) | Store exam material at specific spots in the *actual exam room*, then look at the spots during the test | Same spatial strength, plus his exams are in known rooms | PHYS (Academic 200), ECON (ETC 202). *Not CSE — its exams are online at home, so anchor to his desk instead* |
| **Mnemonic imagery + story** (AoM, ADDA, Stanford) | Turn an arbitrary pairing into a vivid, weird, exaggerated image or one-line story | **Brainstormer** — he generates images and alternatives effortlessly; weirdness is free for him | Vocabulary-heavy loads: ECON terms, Python keywords, physics symbol meanings |
| **Sound-alike keyword** (AoM, ADDA) | Anchor a new word to what it *sounds like*, then link the sound-image to the meaning | Cue User support for pure vocabulary | ECON and TCOM terminology |
| **Chunking** (Stanford) | Hold 3–4 units, not 10 — group items into meaningful clusters first | Working memory is the constraint chunking protects; pairs with his Process Supporter need for external structure | Formula families in physics, argument structures in TCOM |
| **Self-test to overlearned** (ADDA, AoM) | Same-difficulty self-tests until 100% three times in a row — not until "it feels known" | Gives retrieval practice a **hard, countable gate** instead of a feeling | Quiz/exam prep in every course; the standard for "drilled" |
| **Study-before-sleep** (AoM, ADDA) | Put the hardest material last, right before sleep | Free consolidation; fits the calendar — `Ben Bed Time` ends 22:00, `SLEEP` starts 23:00 | The 22:00–23:00 hour on Mon/Wed/Fri/Sat is a natural hardest-item review slot |
| **Movement pairing** (ADDA) | Pair encoding with walking, gesture, or physical manipulation | Former lead carpenter — his strongest memories are motor-anchored; profile lists movement as a named support | Campus gap blocks: walk-and-recite works on the Marietta campus loop |
| **Location rotation** (ADDA) | Study different subjects in different physical spots | Cheap context-cue separation between five courses | Campus: different gap-block spots per subject. Home: even a different chair works |

## Dropped, and why — so nobody re-adds them

- **Alphabet pegs, number-shape pegs, PAO systems** (AoM #7 and similar): competition-grade
  systems for memorizing arbitrary *sequences*. High setup cost, and nothing in a KSU first-year
  load needs 100-digit recall. The simpler image + story techniques cover the same ground.
- **Songs/rhymes as a primary channel** (Stanford, ADDA): nothing in his profile suggests an
  auditory lever; visual-spatial and motor anchors are measured strengths. Use rhyme only when
  one arrives free.
- **Anything framed as "you are a visual learner"**: aptitudes are measured abilities, styles
  are self-reported identities. The vault ruled correctly on 2026-07-12 — techniques above use
  his *measured* spatial strength; they do not assign him an identity.

## The assembly — how a term actually gets locked

This is the sequence the Educator hat runs when a term, formula, or date must stick. It is the
existing Term Anchoring method with the cue-construction step made explicit:

1. **State it** — one exact meaning. Never "it kind of means."
2. **Build the cue** — pick the cheapest that fits: association to something known, a weird
   image, a sound-alike, a spot in a memory palace, or a physical/motor anchor.
3. **Use it immediately** in the live problem.
4. **Self-test it cold** — later same session, then next session, then at the weekly sweep
   (expanding gaps: minutes → a day → a week).
5. **Overlearned gate** for exam material: 100% three times in a row at exam difficulty.

## Working-memory protection (Stanford) — already system policy, now named

Chris's system *is* the "accommodate and outsource" strategy: calendars, `NOW.md`,
`EVENING_READING.md`, and written maps exist to keep working memory free for the actual
problem. Two rules worth keeping explicit during study sessions:

- **3–4 chunks on the table at once, never more.** When a problem needs more, write the rest
  down — the whiteboard is working memory that doesn't decay.
- **Skeleton-first is cognitive-load management**, not a preference. A blank page forces him to
  hold structure *and* content simultaneously; the skeleton holds the structure so his working
  memory can hold the content.
