# Negotiation Toolkit

> Source: Chris Voss & Tahl Raz, *Never Split the Difference* (2016). Migrated from
> FORGE July 7, 2026 — consolidates 12 FORGE chapter-by-chapter pages into one
> practical toolkit. Referenced from [[sales-system|Sales System]] (the discovery
> call, the close, fee negotiation) and [[smb-ai-audit-method|SMB AI Audit Method]]
> (discovery interviews, findings presentations, scope pushback).

## Purpose
Give the sales system and the audit method a concrete, non-generic toolkit for the
moments that actually decide deals: discovery interviews, skeptical first meetings,
findings presentations, fee/scope negotiation, and stalled or silent prospects.

## Key Idea
Voss's core argument: rational, interest-based negotiation (*Getting to Yes*) loses to
real human irrationality. People decide emotionally and rationalize afterward — so a
negotiator's job is to make the other person feel heard and in control, not to win an
argument. Every tool below is a mechanism for doing that. The standard to hold
yourself to: **get what you need and leave the other person feeling like the
relationship is intact** — never "split the difference."

## Practical Actions — The Toolkit

**Mirroring.** Repeat the last 1–3 words the other person said, in an inquisitive tone,
then go silent for at least 4 seconds. Triggers elaboration without you asking a
question. Use throughout discovery interviews to keep an owner talking.

**Labeling.** Name an emotion out loud: "It seems like...", "It sounds like...",
"It looks like..." — never "I feel" or "I'm hearing." Labeling a negative defuses it;
labeling a positive reinforces it. Go silent after delivering a label.

**The Accusation Audit.** Before a difficult conversation (a skeptical first meeting, a
pricing talk, a scope renegotiation), list every negative thing the other person could
say about you and say it yourself, first: *"You might be thinking we're just here to
justify our fee"* / *"You might think we don't understand how your crews actually
work."* Saying it first strips it of power.

**Calibrated Questions.** Open-ended "What" and "How" questions (never "Why" — it reads
as accusatory) that give the other person the illusion of control while you steer.
Standby questions for discovery: *"What's the biggest challenge you're facing?" /
"What's important to you about this?" / "How can we solve this problem?"* For a
stalled deal: *"Have you given up on this project?"* almost always gets an immediate
reply.

**The "That's Right" test.** A quick "Yes" in a findings or pricing meeting is often
Counterfeit (a polite dodge) or Confirmation (a reflex), not real Commitment. Build a
Summary (Label + Paraphrase of their situation) accurate enough to earn a genuine
**"That's right."** A hollow **"You're right"** is a warning sign, not a close — it
means nothing will actually change.

**Guaranteeing follow-through.** A verbal "Yes" isn't the job — implementation is. Ask
*"How will we know we're on track?"* and *"How will we address things if we're off
track?"* before considering an engagement closed. If the answer is "I'll try," that
means "I plan to fail" — dig further with more "How" questions.

**Negotiator types.** People are roughly Analysts (data-driven, hate surprises, want
proof — no fluff), Accommodators (relationship first, will avoid voicing real
objections), or Assertives (direct, need to feel heard before they'll listen). Read the
client's type early and match: data-heavy proposals for Analysts, relationship time
before any ask for Accommodators, a chance to vent before presenting for Assertives.

**The Ackerman Bargaining Model** (fee/price negotiation): set your real target price
→ open at 65% of it → raise in three decreasing steps to 85%, 95%, 100% → use a
precise, non-round final number ($4,890 not $5,000) → add a small non-monetary item on
the final offer to signal you've hit your limit.

**Black Swan triage.** When a client's stated objection doesn't add up or their
behavior looks irrational, it's almost never actually irrational — it's one of three
things: they're **ill-informed**, they're **constrained** (budget/authority they
haven't disclosed), or they have **other interests** you haven't identified yet. Get
face time and watch unguarded moments (before/after a meeting) — that's where hidden
constraints surface.

**Don't compromise on price/scope.** "Splitting the difference" produces the worst
outcome for both sides (Voss's black-shoe/brown-shoe parable). No deal is better than a
bad deal — hold the line on retainer pricing rather than meeting halfway.

**The Negotiation One Sheet** — fill this out before any high-stakes client
conversation (discovery call, scope negotiation, fee/retainer talk):
1. **Goal** — a specific, optimistic, written best-case target (not a fallback).
2. **Summary** — 2–3 sentences on why you're there, what you want, what they want.
3. **Labels/Accusation Audit** — 3–5 prepared labels anticipating objections.
4. **Calibrated Questions** — 3–5 "What"/"How" questions, including ones aimed at
   finding out who else (behind-the-table players) needs to sign off.
5. **Noncash Offers** — what could they give you that's cheap to them, valuable to you?

## Why It Matters
Negotiation is load-bearing in nearly every client interaction the audit business runs
on: the discovery interview, the scope conversation, the findings presentation, the
fee negotiation, the retainer renewal. Generic sales advice doesn't cover any of this —
this toolkit is specific, rehearsable, and immediately usable.

## Beginner Version
Learn and use three tools only: Mirroring, Labeling, and Calibrated Questions ("What"/
"How"). Practice them in every discovery interview before adding anything else.

## Intermediate Version
Add the Accusation Audit before every skeptical first meeting, the "That's Right" test
for reading real buy-in in findings presentations, and negotiator-type reading to shape
how each proposal is structured.

## Advanced Version
Run the full Negotiation One Sheet before every fee/retainer negotiation, use the
Ackerman Model for structured price negotiation, and actively hunt Black Swans in every
stalled or unusually-resistant deal rather than writing the client off as difficult.

## Revenue Connection
Directly protects margin: the Ackerman Model and "don't compromise" discipline hold
retainer pricing instead of eroding it deal by deal. The "That's Right" test prevents
false-positive closes (deals that look sold but never convert to signed work) from
polluting the pipeline math in [[sales-system|Sales System]].

## Human-Agent Management Connection
Calibrated Questions and Labeling are exactly the discovery tools [[smb-ai-audit-method|
the audit method]]'s Step 2 (Business Flow Map) needs to get an owner and frontline
staff talking honestly about where work actually breaks down — not what they think you
want to hear.

## Risks / Failure Modes
- **Treating a quick "Yes" as a close** — the Daryl-call lesson: a counterfeit "Yes"
  said to end an uncomfortable conversation isn't commitment.
- **Splitting the difference on fee or scope** — produces the worst outcome for both
  sides; hold firm or walk.
- **Skipping the Accusation Audit before a skeptical meeting** — unaddressed
  skepticism doesn't go away, it just surfaces later as stalled decisions.
