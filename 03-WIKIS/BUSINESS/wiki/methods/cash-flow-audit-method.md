# Cash Flow Audit Method (Profit First)

> Source: Mike Michalowicz, *Profit First* (2014). Migrated from FORGE July 7, 2026 —
> consolidates 14 FORGE pages into one sellable diagnostic-and-implementation service,
> parallel in structure to [[smb-ai-audit-method|SMB AI Audit Method]] but for cash
> management specifically. A natural second offering alongside the operational audit —
> Gerber's [[owner-dependency-diagnostic|E-Myth]] diagnoses *why* an SMB stays owner-dependent;
> this is the specific financial-systems fix for the symptom that diagnosis almost
> always produces: a business that "makes money" but never accumulates real profit.

## Purpose
Give the business a second, financial-side diagnostic and retainer offering: a
repeatable method for assessing a client's true cash health, fixing it structurally
(not through willpower or better bookkeeping advice), and staying on retainer as their
accountability partner.

## Key Idea
Standard accounting (Sales − Expenses = Profit) treats profit as whatever happens to be
left over — which means it rarely happens, because expenses reliably expand to consume
whatever revenue is available (**Parkinson's Law**, reinforced by the **Recency
Effect**: owners manage off the bank balance, not a cash flow statement). **Profit
First flips the formula: Sales − Profit = Expenses.** Profit, owner's pay, and tax get
physically removed from operating cash *first*, via a small-plates multi-account
system, forcing the business to run efficiently on whatever remains. Same arithmetic,
opposite behavioral result — this is a structural fix, not a budgeting pep talk.

## Practical Actions — The Method

### 1. Diagnose: The Instant Assessment
Pull trailing-twelve-month financials and compute:
- **Real Revenue** = Top Line Revenue − Materials & Subcontractor costs (excludes
  employee labor, unlike gross profit). This is what actually sizes a business — a "$5M"
  contractor paying $4M to subs is really a $1M business.
- Run the six-row worksheet: Top Line, Materials & Subs, Real Revenue, Profit, Owner's
  Pay, Tax, Operating Expenses — the last four must sum back to Real Revenue (a
  built-in error check).
- Compare actuals against Target Allocation Percentages (TAPs) by revenue band (roughly
  5–20% Profit / 0–50% Owner's Pay / 15% Tax / 30–65% Operating Expenses, shifting as
  revenue scales — Owner's Pay % drops, Operating Expenses % grows). The gap per row is
  the **Bleed**; label each "increase" or "decrease" — this converts "things feel
  tight" into specific, dollar-quantified findings.

### 2. Fix: The Four-Account System (plus extensions)
Four accounts minimum — **Profit**, **Owner's Pay**, **Tax**, **Operating Expenses** —
built on four behavioral principles borrowed from diet science: Small Plates (split
every deposit immediately), Serve Sequentially (Profit → Owner's Pay → Tax → whatever's
left for Operating Expenses), Remove Temptation (Profit/Tax accounts at a different,
harder-to-reach bank), Enforce a Rhythm (fixed transfer schedule, not reactive). Add
specialized accounts only once the basic four are stable: Income (separates in from
out), The Vault (3-month emergency reserve), Stocking/Major Equipment (save ahead for
known big purchases), Pass-Through/Materials/Subcontractor (money that was never really
revenue), Drip (retainers — only the earned portion moves to Income), Sales Tax
(never the business's money to begin with).

### 3. Roll Out: Day One
Tell the accountant first (pushback that it's "technically wrong" signals they don't
understand cash-flow behavior, not that the system is flawed). Name the accounts with
their percentages directly in the bank's nickname field. **Start at "historical + 1%"**
— never jump straight to the target percentages; the goal of quarter one is building
the habit, not hitting the number. Run the split on money already in the bank today,
not just future deposits. Cut expenses to match — **no borrowing back from Profit/Tax/
Owner's Pay to cover a shortfall; a shortfall means an expense goes, not that the
allocation gets skipped.**

### 4. Sustain: The 10/25 Rhythm
Pay bills exactly twice a month (10th and 25th). Seven-step sequence each cycle:
deposit revenue → transfer allocation percentages → move sub-account amounts → sweep
Tax/Profit to the harder-to-reach bank → pay owner their allocated salary → pay
employees → pay bills with what's left. **If the seven steps can't complete, that's the
audit finding, surfaced automatically** — the business is screaming that it can't
afford its current expense structure. Quarterly: distribute 50% of the Profit Account
to owners (never returned to the business — no "plowback"), keep 50% as reserve, and
nudge allocation percentages ~3 points toward target.

### 5. Handle Debt Without Pausing the System
Don't delay Profit First until debt clears — that just means the habit never forms and
debt re-accumulates. Instead: keep the normal allocation rhythm, but shift the
quarterly distribution split to **99% debt repayment / 1% owner reward** until debt
clears, then shift back toward normal. Manufacture visible reward around the *saving*
behavior itself (not just the eventual payoff) — pain-avoidance alone doesn't sustain a
habit once the acute pain fades.

### 6. Efficiency Before Sales
Before recommending "sell more" to a cash-strapped client, check efficiency first —
new revenue just scales existing inefficiency. Two directly reusable diagnostics:
- **The Strategex quartile study**: sort a client's customers into revenue quartiles.
  Typically the top quartile produces ~150% of total profit while the bottom quartile
  is a net loss — despite roughly equal service cost per quartile. Fire the bottom,
  clone the top.
- **The Pareto Overlap**: cross client value against offering profitability — which top
  clients buy the most profitable offerings (clone), which top clients buy the least
  profitable (redirect), which weak clients buy profitable offerings anyway (worth
  keeping), which weak clients buy only unprofitable ones (fire, no ambiguity left).

### 7. Build in Accountability
The system's main failure point is the owner's own discipline lapsing, not the
math. Structural fix: a lightweight recurring accountability check-in — a two-person
"Profit Pod" biweekly call (timed the 11th/26th, just after each allocation date):
instant health check-in → 3-minute update → report current account balances
(screenshot, not verbal — removes room to self-deceive) → challenges → wins →
clarifying questions → committed actions → repeat for the other partner. This is a
directly buildable retainer deliverable in itself.

### 8. Re-Audit: The Common Failure-Mode Checklist
For any client already running the system, check five specific, checkable red flags:
1. **Went too big too fast** (allocated 20–30% immediately, then quietly pulled it back) — fix: restart at a small percentage with a scheduled quarterly increase.
2. **Cut the wrong costs** (cut an *asset* — something that increases output per dollar — instead of a pure cost).
3. **"Plowing back" or "reinvesting"** — euphemisms for borrowing from Profit/Tax/Owner's Pay; treat any such transfer as a borrowing event and flag it.
4. **Raiding the Tax Account** — a growing Tax Account balance usually means the accountant's stale estimate hasn't caught up to real, growing profitability, not that the business over-reserved.
5. **Sliding back to conventional accounting advice** — ask any client's accountant "of your client base, what percentage hold real accumulating cash year over year?"; a non-answer is itself diagnostic.

## Why It Matters
Nearly every page in this method is a standalone, buildable deliverable — the Instant
Assessment worksheet, the client-quartile study, the Day One rollout script, the
accountability check-in agenda, the failure-mode re-audit checklist. This is one of the
most directly productizable services in the whole business: diagnosis in one meeting,
implementation in a week, and a natural recurring accountability retainer.

## Beginner Version
Offer the Instant Assessment alone as a low-friction diagnostic ($500–$1,500,
comparable to the [[smb-ai-audit-method|starter diagnostic]]): trailing-twelve-month
numbers in, Real Revenue + Bleed/Fix findings out. No account setup yet.

## Intermediate Version
Full diagnosis-to-implementation engagement: Instant Assessment → four-account setup
→ Day One rollout → 10/25 rhythm handoff, priced as a fixed-fee project alongside
operational audit work.

## Advanced Version
Ongoing accountability retainer (Profit Pod-style biweekly check-ins) plus the advanced
toolkit once a client has 2+ stable quarters: RIFA (Required Income For Allocation —
replaces "monthly nut" thinking by tying required sales directly to desired owner pay),
the Pareto Overlap, and the employee-affordability formula (Real Revenue ÷ an
industry labor multiplier) as a quick overstaffed/understaffed sanity check.

## Revenue Connection
A distinct, sellable service line: diagnostic fee comparable to the operational audit,
implementation as a fixed-fee project, and the accountability check-in as a natural,
low-effort recurring retainer add-on (see [[retainer-model|Retainer Model]]) — cash
management is a permanent need, not a one-time fix, which is exactly the recurring
value a retainer is built to capture.

## Human-Agent Management Connection
The accountability-partner role (running biweekly check-ins, catching the five failure
modes before they compound) is a clean example of the

instead of their AI workflows — same discipline, human-only domain (see

## Risks / Failure Modes
- **Recommending the advanced toolkit too early** — RIFA, the Pareto Overlap, and the
  extended accounts are explicitly gated behind 2+ stable quarters of the basic system;
  introducing them at Day One is the same over-complication risk the audit method's
  own [[risks-and-failure-modes|scope-sprawl]] risk describes.
- **Letting a client's Tax Account balance get "corrected" without an accountant
  check-in** — a growing balance is usually right, not a mistake to fix.
- **Treating this as bookkeeping advice instead of a behavioral system** — the fix is
  structural (account separation, forced rhythm), not another lecture on discipline.
