# Owner-Dependency Diagnostic (E-Myth)

> Source: Michael Gerber, *The E-Myth Revisited* (1995). Migrated from FORGE July 7,
> 2026 — consolidates 17 FORGE pages. This is the *why is this business stuck*
> complement to [[smb-ai-audit-method|SMB AI Audit Method]]'s *what to automate* — use
> both together. Several questions here are ready-to-use verbatim in a discovery
> interview.

## Purpose
Give the audit a vocabulary and a set of observable, non-financial diagnostics for
naming exactly why a small business stays owner-dependent — root cause, growth stage,
target state, and the seven-step build-out to close the gap.

## Key Idea
Most small businesses are started by a **Technician** — someone good at the technical
work — who wrongly assumes that knowing the work means knowing how to build a business
around it (the **Fatal Assumption**). The fix isn't more technical skill; it's building
the business as a **Franchise Prototype**: systems-dependent, not people-dependent,
designed as if 5,000 identical copies had to run as smoothly as the first — whether or
not the client ever actually franchises.

## Practical Actions

### 1. Diagnose Root Cause — the Fatal Assumption
Ask: *"What does a typical week look like for you?"* If the answer is dominated by
hands-on tasks rather than planning, hiring, or systems-building, the Fatal Assumption
is active. Benchmark against Gerber's field data: the typical owner splits roughly
**10% Entrepreneur / 20% Manager / 70% Technician**. Watch for personal-name signage
("Joe's Place"), the owner physically taking over work mid-task ("give it to me, I'll
do it"), and employees routing every decision back to the owner — all directly
observable on a single site visit.

Read which internal personality dominates by listening to language: **Entrepreneur**
("I wonder... what if...", future-oriented), **Manager** (process/procedure language,
past-oriented, craves order), **Technician** (task/doing language, present-oriented,
"if you want it done right, do it yourself" — by far the most common in SMBs).

### 2. Diagnose Stage — Infancy / Adolescence / Maturity
Stage is about **operating model, not company age or size**. Maturity isn't the end of
a natural progression — mature companies were *designed* that way from the start.

- **Infancy**: owner = business (remove the owner, the business disappears); rising
  hours with falling consistency (the "Master Juggler" pattern — ask about hours worked
  *and* recent customer complaints, look for both trending the wrong way together).
- **Adolescence**: begins when the owner gets help — always technical help first, never
  managerial. Ask: *was the first hire planned, or reactive to a crisis?* Three failure
  exits, all audit-detectable: **Getting Small Again** (a key employee who'd absorbed
  undocumented responsibility leaves, business contracts rather than systematizes),
  **Going for Broke** (growth outpaces systems and cash), **Adolescent Survival** (the
  business survives but consumes the owner — visible burnout, can't take a day off).
- **Maturity**: the single most useful audit question — *"Does this business have any
  written plan — a model of how it's supposed to work, independent of you — or is
  everything in your head?"* Almost no one passes this test, which makes it low value as
  a differentiator but high value as a report framing: "your business was never given a
  model to grow into" is less indicting and more buildable than "your business has
  problems."

### 3. Test for Management by Abdication (Key-Person Risk)
Distinguish **delegation** (employee knows why the work matters, what result they're
accountable for, and the standard it's measured against) from **abdication** (owner
hands off a task with relief, then disengages — no training to a standard). Ask the
owner *how* they trained their first hires; ask employees *what standard* their work is
judged against. "I just showed them and let them go" + "don't get complaints" = abdication.

**Key-person risk test**: *"Can this business keep operating normally for more than one
week if [employee] is unreachable?"* A "no" is a single point of failure flag, directly
caused by abdication rather than documented delegation.

### 4. Target State — The Franchise Prototype (Six Rules)
The prescriptive half of the audit — the six-point checklist for what to recommend
building toward, testable directly against the client's current operation:
1. **Consistent value**, delivered the same way every time — not just good, *predictable*.
2. **Operated by people with the lowest possible skill level** (the Rule of Ordinary
   People) — if the business needs rare, highly skilled people to function, it can't be
   reliably systematized.
3. **Impeccable order** — does the physical environment signal competence to a stranger?
4. **Documented in Operations Manuals** — "this is how we do it here," written down, not
   living only in someone's head.
5. **Uniformly predictable service** — would a repeat customer know what to expect in
   detail, before walking in?
6. **Uniform visual presentation** — branding, uniforms, signage read as one coherent business.

Then run the five self-audit questions directly with the owner (best used once rapport
is established, not in the first five minutes): *How can I get my business to work
without me? How can I get my people to work without my constant interference? How can I
systematize this so the 5,000th copy runs as smoothly as the first? How can I own this
business and still be free of it? How can I spend my time on work I love, not work I
have to do?* An honest "I don't know" to any of these **is** the finding.

### 5. The Build Engine — Innovation, Quantification, Orchestration
The Prototype isn't built once — it's maintained through three activities, applied
continuously: **Innovate** how the business finds and keeps customers (not the
commodity itself — a reworded retail greeting alone produced a measured 10–16% sales
lift in Gerber's example). **Quantify** every innovation against a baseline — ask "how
many selling opportunities did you have yesterday?"; most owners can't answer, which
means nothing in the business can be evaluated. **Orchestrate**: once proven, do it the
same way every time, eliminating discretion at the point of delivery — this is not
about removing craft judgment, only about locking in what's already proven to work.

### 6. The Seven-Step Program (Build-Out Order)
1. **Primary Aim** — the owner's personal life vision (better suited to a retainer-stage
   conversation than a first walkthrough: "what do you want your life to look like?").
2. **Strategic Objective** — money standard ("if you sold this business in five years,
   what would it need to be worth?"), the **Opportunity Worth Pursuing** viability
   filter (also a client-screening tool for Chris's own pipeline), and the
   **commodity-vs-product** distinction: the commodity is what the customer walks out
   with; the product is what they *feel*. Ask "what do you actually sell, separate from
   materials and labor?"
3. **Organizational Strategy** — diagnose personality-based org chaos with: *"For these
   recent problems, who specifically was accountable?"* "Everyone, so no one" is the
   tell. Fix: build the org chart for the finished business (not current headcount),
   write a **Position Contract** for every box (a signed accountability agreement, not a
   job description) — a directly buildable retainer deliverable.
4. **Management Strategy** — a Management System is a marketing tool, not primarily an
   employee-control tool. Ask: *"What happens for a repeat customer the second time
   they're served — is anything remembered and automatically delivered, or does every
   visit start from zero?"*
5. **People Strategy** — you can't force compliance; give people a "game worth playing"
   communicated *before* they start, not after. Test: does the owner personally model
   the standard they ask of crew? A structured hiring process (group presentation of the
   philosophy → individual interviews → structured first day) is a buildable retainer deliverable.
6. **Marketing Strategy** — buying decisions are made unconsciously in seconds and
   rationalized afterward; low conversion is usually a signal problem (truck signage,
   phone manner, estimate delivery), not a pitch problem. Ask the owner to state their
   business's "It" (its one-sentence purpose, à la FedEx's "absolutely, positively
   overnight") — if they can't, there's no standard for staff to be measured against.
7. **Systems Strategy** — triage every observed problem into **Hard** (physical/
   environmental fix needed), **Soft** (script/process fix needed), or **Information**
   (owner literally can't see what's happening — build a tracking tool). A recurring
   quality complaint that's survived multiple rounds of "talking to the crew about it"
   is a Hard System problem in Soft System's clothing — fix the environment, not the
   person.

### 7. Structure the Report — the Gap Method
Gerber's own definition of an audit: *"Analyze the business as it is today, decide what
it should look like when finished, and the gap between the two tells you exactly what
needs to be done."* Use this as the literal report structure: current state → target
state (the six Franchise Prototype rules) → the gap → the specific missing system.

Name the **Comfort Zone** explicitly during the retainer-sales conversation — the
psychological mechanism that makes an owner agree enthusiastically with every finding,
then quietly revert within weeks. Naming it in advance ("what will you likely fall back
into under stress?") turns the risk into the argument for ongoing check-ins rather than
a one-time report.

### 8. Watch for the Survival Trap (Crisis-Driven Decisions)
A related pattern, common in cash-strapped clients: **Point A** is the crisis, **Point
B** is the owner's vision, and under pressure the owner reaches for whatever relieves
immediate pain (an ill-fitting project, a discount, doing everything personally) —
moves that feel productive but run sideways or backward relative to Point B. Ask:
*"Walk me through the last three decisions you made when cash was tight."* A string of
individually-defensible "smart moves" with no growing cash cushion and no clearer
specialization is the tell. See [[cash-flow-audit-method|Cash Flow Audit Method]] for
the structural fix (bank balance accounting is the underlying mechanism that sustains
this trap).

## Why It Matters
This is the single most directly usable diagnostic toolkit for the front half of an
audit — nearly every question above is verbatim-usable in a real client conversation,
and the Gap Method gives the report itself a proven structure.

## Beginner Version
Run the Fatal Assumption checklist and the stage diagnosis (Infancy/Adolescence/
Maturity) on the first site visit — both are observable without financial records.

## Intermediate Version
Add the full six-rule Franchise Prototype target-state comparison and the Position
Contract / Hard-Soft-Information triage as report sections.

## Advanced Version
Run the full seven-step build-out as a structured retainer engagement, with Position
Contracts, a documented hiring process, and a sales-funnel tracking tool (the 13-point
Benchmark table: calls made → prospects reached → appointments → needs analyses →
solutions presentations → sold, with dollar values) as concrete deliverables.

## Revenue Connection
The Opportunity Worth Pursuing filter doubles as a client-screening tool for Chris's
own pipeline. Position Contracts, the hiring-process template, and the sales-funnel
tracker are all directly sellable retainer deliverables, not just diagnostic
frameworks.

## Human-Agent Management Connection
The Rule of Ordinary People (systems, not rare talent, should carry the business) is
the same design principle behind [[human-agent-operating-model|AI-first work]]: build
the system so an average hire — or an AI doing first-pass production — can produce a
consistent result, with the owner's judgment reserved for what the system can't yet handle.

## Risks / Failure Modes
- **Pushing the five self-audit questions too early** — they're confronting; use once
  rapport is established, not in the first five minutes.
- **Skipping straight to Systems Strategy** — it's the final, integrating step of the
  seven-step program; recommending Operations Manuals before the Strategic Objective and
  Org Strategy are settled gives the client nothing to standardize toward.
- **Not naming the Comfort Zone** — the single most common cause of an enthusiastically
  received audit that produces no lasting change.
