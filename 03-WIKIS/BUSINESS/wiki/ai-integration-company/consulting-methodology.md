# Consulting Methodology (Flawless Consulting)

> Source: Peter Block, *Flawless Consulting* (2nd ed.). Migrated from FORGE July 7,
> 2026 — consolidates 22 FORGE pages, the richest single source in the migration.
> This is the engagement-mechanics layer underneath
> [[fulfillment-system|Fulfillment System]] and
> [[smb-ai-audit-method|SMB AI Audit Method]]: not what to deliver, but how to run
> every conversation that gets you there — contracting, discovery, feedback,
> implementation.

## Purpose
Give every client conversation a proven structure: how to contract for the work, how
to discover what's actually wrong, how to deliver hard findings without triggering
defensiveness, and how to get recommendations actually implemented rather than politely
agreed to and ignored.

## Key Idea
A consultant has **influence, not authority** — no direct power to implement anything.
The moment you take direct responsibility for the work itself, you're acting as a
manager, not a consultant, and you've fallen into the **surrogate manager trap**: doing
the fix yourself produces a one-time result with nothing learned, and nothing that
survives you leaving. Every technique below serves one goal: build the client's own
capability, not your own indispensability.

Every engagement — ten minutes or ten months — moves through five phases: **Entry &
Contracting → Discovery & Dialogue → Feedback & Decision → Engagement & Implementation
→ Extension, Recycle, or Termination.** Skipping a phase's business doesn't skip the
problem — it resurfaces later as unexplained resistance.

## Practical Actions

### Phase 1 — Contracting
**The nine things a contract (verbal or written) should cover**: the boundaries of the
analysis, the project's objectives, what information you need, your role (state the
50/50 split explicitly), the specific product you'll deliver, what support you need
from the client, the time schedule, confidentiality (who sees the report), and whether
you want feedback on impact later.

**The eight-step contracting meeting**: (1) personal acknowledgement — a genuine
statement of how you feel being there, not small talk; (2) restate your understanding
of the problem, reassure tentatively; (3) ask the client's wants directly — "what do
you want from me?"; (4) state your own wants (essential vs. desirable), plainly, then
go quiet; (5) reach agreement and say so out loud; (6) test control and commitment —
"is this something you really want?" / "do you feel you have enough control over how
this proceeds?"; (7) give genuine support for the risk the client is taking; (8)
restate exactly what each of you will do next.

**If wants-and-offers negotiation stalls**: name it ("we seem to be stuck"), propose a
different combination of terms, and if it's still stuck, shift the conversation from
the project's content to the process itself ("how do we feel we're doing in reaching
agreement?") — this usually surfaces the real concern (control, trust) hiding behind
the stated one. If it's still stuck after that, say so directly and end it — not every
project was meant to happen, and pushing a bad contract through is worse than declining it.

**Selling is the same sequence reversed**: state the problem you believe the prospect
has, check if you're right, then state your want (the engagement) before asking theirs
— pretending you're "just there to help" when you're also selling reads as inauthentic.
**"No budget" and "no time" are usually a motivation problem in disguise** — managers
find time and money for things they actually want.

**Fourteen ground rules worth internalizing**: responsibility is 50/50 or the
relationship collapses; all wants are legitimate; you can say no to what a client wants
from you; you don't always get what you want and you'll survive; you can't contract
with someone who's not in the room (get key stakeholders in the conversation before
assuming their support); write it down when you can, since most contracts break from
neglect, not intent; social contracts are always renegotiable.

### Phase 2 — Discovery (Not "Diagnosis")
Reject the medical model (an outside expert analyzes and prescribes) — human systems
need the client to find their own answer and own the implementation, or it won't stick.
**Never accept the presenting problem as the real problem.** A classic case: a company
couldn't retain new hires; management assumed pay and housing, ran a salary survey,
turnover got worse. Real cause, found by actually interviewing new hires directly: no
real assignment for a year, no feedback, overloaded supervisors. Redefining the
presenting problem is often the single most valuable thing a consultant does —
independent of any technical expertise.

**Three layers of analysis for any interview**: (1) the presenting problem, in the
person's own words; (2) what *others* are doing to cause or maintain it; (3) the
person's own role — by action or by simply not attending to it. Layer 3 matters most:
it moves responsibility from external forces back to whoever actually has power to act.
If someone claims no role at all, ask: *"Suppose you did know what your role was — what
would it be?"*

**Assess how the problem is being managed, not just the technical problem itself** —
every technical finding has an organizational-management half (defensive information
control, distrust between departments, unclear authority) that gets skipped because
"we were asked to solve X, not comment on how you run things." Skipping it means your
technical recommendations get distorted by the very dynamics you didn't name.

**Group norms, not the leader alone, usually explain a stuck team.** "Just fire the
manager" is a trap — replacing a blamed leader buys six months of onboarding, not a
fix, because the group's own patterns (how it handles conflict, support, risk) caused
and will outlast any one leader.

### Phase 3 — Understanding and Naming Resistance
Resistance is not a logical objection to your data — it's an emotional reaction to
being helped and to facing a **difficult reality** the client has been avoiding (someone
underperforming, a political risk, their own management style). It disguises itself as:
demanding more and more detail, flooding you with irrelevant history, blaming timing,
accusing you of impracticality, total silence, total frictionless compliance, or a
sudden mysterious improvement right before a hard finding is due ("flight into
health"). **Trace it to one of two concerns: control or vulnerability.**

**The three-step method — don't fight it, let it burn out:**
1. **Identify the form** — trust nonverbal cues over words; trust your own boredom or
   irritation as a signal; watch for the same explanation repeated a third time.
2. **Name it in neutral, nonpunishing language** — "You're giving me more detail than I
   need — how would you describe it in one sentence?" / "You seem very quiet, I don't
   know how to read that." Imagine what you'd say to a close friend, then say that.
3. **Go quiet.** The strong temptation is to keep talking after naming it — don't. Let
   the client respond; taking them off the hook removes their own responsibility to react.

**Two good-faith answers to any repeated question; the third repetition is resistance**
— name it instead of answering again. And it's not always resistance: a client who says
plainly "no, I don't want to do this" is exercising a legitimate right to decide, not
resisting — respect it as-is.

### Phase 4 — Delivering Feedback
**Condensing the data**: read your notes once, then set them aside and list what you
remember — usually four or five items. What stuck in your memory is what will land with
the client too; don't clutter the meeting with a full inventory. Prefer items the
client can actually act on.

**Two collusion traps to avoid**: don't let the client externalize the cause onto
something outside their control (it feels kind, but leaves them helpless); don't
downplay a difficult relationship you can see is part of the problem just because it's
uncomfortable to name.

**Language: descriptive, not evaluative.** "The owner does about 80% of the talking in
crew meetings" lands; "the owner dominates and doesn't run meetings well" triggers
defensiveness. Avoid judging words (weak, incompetent, dictatorial) and vague
stereotypes. State what *is*, not what *ought to be*.

**The ten-step feedback meeting (scale the percentages to your meeting length):**
1. Restate the original contract (5%).
2. Present findings and recommendations — **hard cutoff at the 20% mark**; the most
   common failure is spending the whole meeting on data because discovery is genuinely
   interesting to both sides.
3. Ask for reactions to both (30% — the heart of the meeting). Don't get defensive; some
   tension is healthy, none means the findings don't matter to anyone.
4. **At the halfway point, ask "Are you getting what you want?"** — the single highest-
   leverage insurance question in the whole meeting; asked with only 5 minutes left, it's
   too late to fix anything.
5. Move to the decision on what to do (30%) — stay in the room for this; ask to be
   included if a client tries to exclude you from it.
6. Test for control and commitment, give support, close with exactly what each side does next (10%).

Present yourself as a **Witness** (accurate, observed, no personal stake in the
verdict) — not a Judge, Jury, Prosecutor, or Defendant. If a client treats you like
you're on trial, that's their resistance, not a verdict on you.

### Phase 5 — Implementation and Engagement (Not Installation)
**Installation fails**: vision statements handed down, standards imposed, rewards tied
to compliance, common training rolled out, everything measured — all five levers are
control instruments, and organizations aren't machines that accept an install.
**"Deciding doesn't get it done"** — a decision is the start of a negotiation with
everyone who has to live with it, not the end of one.

**Engagement works instead**: build real commitment through participation, not
broadcast. **The meeting is the message** — you cannot announce more participation
through a one-way lecture. Concretely: keep any single speaker's monologue short (~15
minutes); create a real platform for public doubt, not just curated good news (people
already say what they really think — just privately, at the water cooler); put genuine,
unpackaged choices on the table rather than a pre-finished plan seeking buy-in; watch
for "familiar refrains" that signal stalling ("the culture has to change first," "we
need more training," "leadership has to model it") — these are avoidance dressed as
analysis, not real diagnoses; and don't underestimate the room itself — round tables and
circles produce more real dialogue than a lecture-hall layout.

**The consultant's own authentic behavior is the highest-leverage tool in
implementation** — clients learn more from what you do than from what you say. **The
single sharpest failure mode of this entire business model**: becoming the surrogate
manager during implementation — doing the fix yourself instead of building the client's
own capacity to run it, which trades short-term satisfaction for zero lasting change
and signals to the whole organization that outside control, not their own, is how
things get fixed.

## Why It Matters
This is the mechanics underneath every phase of an audit-retainer engagement — the
contracting conversation that either sets a solid foundation or a shaky one, the
discovery interviews that either surface the real problem or just confirm the presenting
one, the findings meeting that either lands or triggers defensiveness, and the
implementation phase where most well-diagnosed engagements actually fail.

## Beginner Version
Run the eight-step contracting meeting and the three-layer discovery interview on every
early engagement — both are usable nearly verbatim from day one.

## Intermediate Version
Add the full ten-step feedback meeting agenda and the resistance-naming discipline;
start tracking which of the "familiar refrains" show up during implementation handoffs.

## Advanced Version
Design real engagement structures (public doubt, genuine choice, peer-based
commitment-without-negotiation) into multi-stakeholder rollout meetings for larger
retainer clients — see the source material's eight concrete meeting structures for
specifics when that scale of engagement arrives.

## Revenue Connection
The contracting meeting is the highest-leverage early moment in every engagement — a
solid contract prevents scope disputes and sets up the retainer conversation later. The
feedback meeting's "are you getting what you want?" question directly protects
conversion from audit to implementation project.

## Human-Agent Management Connection
The surrogate-manager warning is the consulting-methodology mirror of
[[human-role-redesign|human role redesign]]'s core argument: the goal is never to do the
work for the client (or replace their people with AI wholesale) — it's building a
system, human-run or AI-assisted, that the client's own team can operate after you leave.

## Risks / Failure Modes
- **Becoming the surrogate manager** — see above; the single most tempting and most
  damaging failure mode for exactly this business model.
- **Skipping the "are you getting what you want?" check** — asked too late (or not at
  all), a feedback meeting can go sideways with no time left to recover.
- **Treating resistance as an argument to win** — fighting it head-on intensifies it;
  naming it and going quiet lets it burn out.
- **Installing instead of engaging during rollout** — a beautifully packaged SOP binder
  nobody actually uses is the direct result of skipping real participation.
