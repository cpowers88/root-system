# Concept — Scalar vs. Vector

## What it is

A **scalar** is a physical quantity described completely by a single number and a unit. It has magnitude only, no direction.

A **vector** is a physical quantity that requires both a magnitude AND a direction to be completely described.

## Real situations that show the difference

- "The car went 60 mph" — speed is a scalar.
- "The car went 60 mph north" — velocity is a vector.
- "The displacement from home to school is 2.0 km" is incomplete — you need "2.0 km northeast" to place the school.
- Temperature, mass, and energy are scalars. Displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, and momentum are vectors.

## How to recognize them

| Scalars | Vectors |
|---|---|
| Temperature (25°C) | Displacement (5 m east) |
| Mass (2.0 kg) | Velocity (30 m/s north) |
| Speed (30 m/s) | Acceleration (9.8 m/s² downward) |
| Energy (100 J) | Force (50 N at 30° above horizontal) |
| Time (3.5 s) | Momentum (mv, with direction) |
| Distance (traveled path length) | Weight (force of gravity, downward) |

## Why direction matters

Two forces of equal magnitude but opposite directions cancel to zero. Add the same two forces as if they were scalars and you get twice the force — completely wrong. Direction is not an optional add-on.

## Notation

Vectors are written with an arrow: **v⃗**, **F⃗**, **a⃗**, or in bold **v**, **F**, **a**.
Scalar (magnitude of a vector): |v⃗| = v, written without the arrow.

## Common Mistake

Using "velocity" and "speed" interchangeably. Speed is the magnitude of velocity — a scalar. Velocity includes direction — a vector. Same confusion applies to "distance" (scalar) vs. "displacement" (vector).
