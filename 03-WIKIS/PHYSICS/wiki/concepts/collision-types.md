# Concept: Collision Types

## The Three Types

| Type | Momentum | Kinetic Energy | Objects After |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elastic | Conserved ✓ | Conserved ✓ | Separate, bounce |
| Inelastic | Conserved ✓ | NOT conserved | Separate, KE lost |
| Perfectly Inelastic | Conserved ✓ | NOT conserved (max loss) | Stick together |

## Key Point

Momentum is conserved in ALL three types (as long as the system is isolated). The difference is always about kinetic energy.

---

## Elastic Collisions

Kinetic energy is fully conserved — no energy is converted to heat, sound, or deformation.

Real examples: Billiard balls (approximately elastic), atomic/molecular collisions.

Equations to use: momentum + kinetic energy conservation (two equations for two unknowns).

Special case: Two equal masses → they exchange velocities. Ball 1 stops; Ball 2 moves at Ball 1's original speed. (Newton's Cradle.)

---

## Inelastic Collisions

Objects separate after the collision, but kinetic energy is lost (converted to heat, sound, deformation). Momentum is still conserved.

Real examples: Car accidents, hitting a baseball, most real-world collisions.

Equations to use: momentum conservation only. The energy loss is often a *given* or computed from KE before and after.

---

## Perfectly Inelastic Collisions

Objects stick together — maximum kinetic energy loss for a given initial momentum.

Real examples: Clay balls that stick, bullet embedding in a block, train cars coupling.

Equation to use:

```
m₁v₁ᵢ + m₂v₂ᵢ = (m₁ + m₂)vf
```

One equation, one unknown.

---

## How to Recognize the Type (from the problem statement)

| Problem says... | Type |
|---|---|
| "Sticks together," "embeds," "couples," "merges" | Perfectly inelastic |
| "Bounces off without energy loss," "elastic" | Elastic |
| "Kinetic energy lost = X" | Inelastic (energy given) |
| "Collision" only (no other info) | Often inelastic — check |

---

## Physical World Anchor: Why Can't All Collisions Be Elastic?

In macroscopic (everyday-scale) collisions, objects deform, atoms vibrate, heat is generated. Perfect elasticity would require no deformation at all. Only at the atomic/molecular scale do truly elastic collisions occur (and even then, molecules have vibration modes). In everyday physics, "elastic" means "close enough that energy loss is negligible."

---

## Common Confusion

"Perfectly inelastic" does NOT mean both objects stop. It means they stick together and move as one object. They stop only if the total initial momentum happens to be zero (e.g., head-on collision between equal masses moving at equal speeds).
