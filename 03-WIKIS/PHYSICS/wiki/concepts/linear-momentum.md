# Concept: Linear Momentum

## What It Is

Linear momentum is the product of an object's mass and velocity:

```
p⃗ = mv⃗
```

It is a **vector** — direction matters, and it matches the direction of velocity. Two cars of the same mass moving in opposite directions have momenta that cancel each other out; they do not add.

## Real-World Anchor

A bowling ball rolling slowly has more momentum than a tennis ball moving fast — mass wins here. But a bullet has far more momentum than it "looks like" because of its enormous speed. Momentum captures both aspects of motion.

## Why Physicists Use Momentum

Momentum is the quantity that doesn't change in isolated systems. Force, velocity, and kinetic energy all change during a collision — momentum does not (if external forces are absent). This makes it the right tool for analyzing brief, chaotic interactions like collisions.

## Connection to Newton's 2nd Law

Newton's original formulation: ΣF⃗ = dp⃗/dt. Force is the rate of change of momentum. F = ma is the special case when mass is constant.

## Units

kg·m/s (also written N·s, since 1 N·s = 1 kg·m/s)

## Quantities That Change Momentum

Only an external force can change an object's momentum. Internal forces (like the collision force between two colliding objects) change each object's individual momentum, but leave the total momentum of the system unchanged.

## Common Confusion

Momentum (p = mv) and kinetic energy (KE = ½mv²) are different things:
- Momentum is a vector; kinetic energy is a scalar.
- Momentum is always conserved in isolated systems; kinetic energy is only conserved in elastic collisions.
- Two identical objects moving in opposite directions have zero total momentum but nonzero total kinetic energy.
