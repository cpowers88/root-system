# Equation: Linear Momentum — p⃗ = mv⃗

## Equation

```
p⃗ = mv⃗
```

## Plain-English Meaning

An object's linear momentum is the product of its mass and velocity. It tells you how hard it is to stop an object: a heavier or faster object has more momentum and requires a larger impulse to change its motion.

## Variables and Units

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| p⃗ | linear momentum | kg·m/s |
| m | mass (scalar) | kg |
| v⃗ | velocity (vector) | m/s |

Momentum is a **vector** — it has the same direction as velocity.

## When to Use

- Anytime you need the "quantity of motion" for an object
- Before setting up any collision or explosion problem
- When applying the impulse-momentum theorem
- As the input to Newton's 2nd law: ΣF⃗ = dp⃗/dt

## When Not to Use

- When you want kinetic energy instead (KE = ½mv² — that's a scalar)
- For rotational problems: use angular momentum L = Iω instead

## Assumptions

- Mass is constant (relativistic mechanics allows mass to change, but that's beyond this course)

## Calculus Origin

Newton's 2nd law in its original and general form is ΣF⃗ = dp⃗/dt. When mass is constant, dp/dt = m(dv/dt) = ma, giving us the familiar F = ma. Momentum is the more fundamental quantity.

## Example Problem Type

A 1500 kg car moves at 25 m/s. What is its momentum?

Solution: p = mv = (1500 kg)(25 m/s) = 37,500 kg·m/s in the direction of travel.

## Common Mistake

Momentum is a vector — direction matters. Two objects with the same speed moving in opposite directions have momenta that cancel when summed, not add. Always include the sign/direction when working with total momentum of a system.
