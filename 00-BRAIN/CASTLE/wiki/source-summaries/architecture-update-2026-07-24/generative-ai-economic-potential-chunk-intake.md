# *The Economic Potential of Generative AI* — Chunk Intake

## Source Identity and Limits

- McKinsey Global Institute report, June 2023.
- 68 physical PDF pages.
- Useful for economic consequence, work-activity decomposition, adoption
  constraints, workforce transition, and value-measurement design.
- It predates current agent systems and fast-moving model economics. Its dollar,
  adoption, and timing estimates are dated scenario outputs, not current facts or
  `.ROOT` revenue evidence.
- It is a consultancy model, not an independently replicated causal study.

## Chunk Findings

### MCK-01 — Physical pages 1–12

- The report defines a use case as a targeted application to a specific business
  challenge that produces one or more measurable outcomes (pp. 7–9). This is a
  stronger unit for `.ROOT` than “AI idea,” “tool,” or “generated artifact.”
- Its two analysis lenses are complementary: named organizational use cases and
  the underlying activities performed by workers (pp. 8–10). A workflow can
  therefore be modeled both as an outcome-bearing intervention and as component
  activities/capabilities.
- The headline $2.6–$4.4 trillion annual use-case estimate and $6.1–$7.9
  trillion broader estimate are scenario outputs, not realized value (pp. 3,
  9–10). `.ROOT` must not translate opportunity estimates into demand, revenue,
  or proof.
- The report separates current technical capability from adoption and states
  that workforce support and redeployment are required for productivity gains
  (p. 3). This supports separate capability, use, outcome, and value states.

### MCK-02 — Physical pages 13–24

- Four functions account for roughly three-quarters of the report's modeled
  use-case value: customer operations, marketing/sales, software engineering,
  and R&D (p. 12). This is a prioritization signal, not a command to force
  `.ROOT` into those markets.
- The “virtual expert” pattern uses natural-language dialogue to retrieve
  organizational knowledge, with a human refining the research (p. 13). That
  supports index-first AI assistance plus human steering, but does not prove
  that a compiled wiki alone is sufficient.
- In the cited customer-support study, less-experienced agents improved more
  than experts; expert performance sometimes did not improve (p. 15). AI
  scaffolding should adapt to demonstrated frontier instead of assuming equal
  benefit at every skill level.
- Content drafting can improve speed and consistency, while strategic thinking,
  intellectual property, bias, and brand-specific judgment still require human
  oversight (pp. 17–18).
- The software lifecycle examples span inception, design, coding, testing, and
  maintenance (pp. 20–21). AI assistance is not a “coding” stage; it must return
  evidence across the full lifecycle.
- The report says architecture quality still largely depends on software
  architects rather than initial AI drafts (p. 21). Draft generation is not
  design proof.

### MCK-03 — Physical pages 25–36

- Product/R&D value comes from generating more candidate designs, testing more
  efficiently, and pairing generative methods with domain simulation—not from
  accepting generated designs without validation (pp. 23–24).
- Multimodal inputs matter where the work itself is visual, spatial, physical,
  or design-based (p. 24). A Markdown-first vault should preserve links to
  diagrams, models, datasets, code, and physical evidence rather than flatten
  every source into prose.
- Construction is estimated as an affected sector, but the report supplies no
  specific validated construction offer or customer demand (p. 25). It is a
  lead for workflow investigation, not market proof.
- Industry spotlights repeatedly require human-in-the-loop quality checks,
  traceability to source data, explainability, and privacy protection
  (pp. 27–30). Source-linked claims and approval gates are operational
  requirements, not documentation polish.
- The report models occupations as about 2,100 detailed work activities mapped
  to 18 required capabilities (pp. 32–34). `.ROOT` should diagnose work at the
  activity/capability level before selecting automation or training.

### MCK-04 — Physical pages 37–48

- Technical potential is not adoption. Adoption depends on solution integration,
  economics, diffusion, regulation, investment, and management decisions
  (pp. 36–39).
- The report explicitly warns that its analysis covers current activities,
  first-order effects, and expert estimates; it does not forecast new work or
  equate automatable activity share with job loss (p. 39).
- Generative AI most changes knowledge activities involving expertise,
  collaboration, decision making, language, and communication (pp. 39–43).
  These are high-value targets for human-AI workflow design, but also the areas
  where judgment and verification matter most.
- Productivity gain assumes displaced hours are redeployed at least as
  productively as before (pp. 44–46). “Time saved” is not value until the saved
  capacity produces a verified better outcome.
- Workforce transition is part of implementation. A system that automates an
  activity without retraining, role redesign, or a destination for freed
  capacity has not completed the value loop (p. 46).

### MCK-05 — Physical pages 49–60

- Risk classes include fairness, intellectual property, privacy, security,
  explainability, reliability, workforce impact, and environmental/social
  impact (pp. 48–49). These should be selected by use-case risk, not copied as
  ceremony into every project.
- The report frames business questions around value capture, workforce
  transition, harmful uses, and transparent sharing of scaling experience
  (p. 49). Implementation reports should record both outcome and transition
  cost.
- Its bottom-up value method first identifies specific use cases, maps affected
  functions/industries, and estimates productivity against current spend
  (pp. 53–55). This is more defensible than starting with a product and inventing
  a benefit afterward.
- Adoption modeling uses four distinct phases: technical feasibility, solution
  development, economic feasibility, and end-user adoption (pp. 58–60). CASTLE
  should not collapse these into a single “ready” status.
- Capability scoring combined automated keyword mapping with manual correction
  for anomalies and nuance (p. 58). Machine classification needs exception
  review where context changes the meaning.

### MCK-06 — Physical pages 61–68

- Economic feasibility compares solution cost with human labor cost, but the
  authors acknowledge that quality, efficiency, and error reduction can create
  value not captured by labor substitution alone (p. 59). `.ROOT` should measure
  multiple operational outcomes rather than only hours or headcount.
- Adoption can be limited by integration difficulty, talent, organizational
  structure, regulation, and user acceptance (p. 60). A technical build without
  an operating model is incomplete.
- Productivity estimates assume labor returns at the same productivity level
  and exclude general-equilibrium effects (pp. 60–61). Treat the numerical
  forecasts as bounded scenarios.
- The research was funded and controlled by McKinsey partners and supported by
  internal experts plus external advisers (pp. 62–63). It is useful
  decision-model evidence with an institutional consultancy perspective.

## Architecture-Update Returns

### Keep

- Specific problem → activity decomposition → capability requirement → bounded
  intervention → measurable outcome.
- Separate technical feasibility, solution development, economic feasibility,
  adoption, and observed value.
- Human steering, source traceability, exception review, and risk-sized gates.

### Modify

- Add an **activity/capability map** to the intake-to-build path when the work is
  consequential or cross-functional.
- Require every “time saved” claim to name how freed capacity was redeployed and
  what outcome changed.
- Distinguish opportunity estimate, implemented use, operational outcome,
  willingness to pay, and revenue in CASTLE status.

### Do Not Infer

- The 2023 market estimates are not current prices, demand, or revenue evidence.
- Industry exposure is not proof that Chris should target that industry.
- Technical automation potential is not adoption, implementation success, job
  replacement, or human capability.
